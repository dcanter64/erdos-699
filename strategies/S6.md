# Strategy S6: Frontier-Guided Analysis

Erdos Problem #699, Cycle 1, strategy round. Agent S6.
Problem: for $1 \le i < j \le n/2$, $C(n,i)$ and $C(n,j)$ share a prime
factor $p \ge i$; equivalently $\gcd(V_i(n), C(n,j)) > 1$, where
$V_i(n)$ is the part of $C(n,i)$ supported on primes $\ge i$.
Established inputs used: the Phase 0 reduction (a counterexample
outside the 12 EEES78 exception pairs needs $2j \le n \le N(i,j)$,
$N(i,j) = \max\{n : C(n,i) < C(2j, 2(j-i))\}$), the closure for
$j \le 3i/2$, and the cap-10000 sweep with its 768,741 fully cleared
pairs (RESULTS.md, pairpass.json).

## Headline findings

1. **$i = 1$ and $i = 2$ are closed completely, elementarily, for all
   $j$ and all $n$** (Section 2, full proofs). No EEES78 input, no
   exceptions, no sweep needed. The reason is structural: for
   $i \le 2$, $V_i(n) = C(n,i)$ identically (every prime is $\ge i$),
   so the identity lemma of Phase 0 applies to the whole of $C(n,i)$,
   not just to its large-prime part. The entire difficulty of #699
   lives in $i \ge 3$, where the small primes $2, \dots, i-1$ can
   absorb the common factor.
2. **The cap frontier is a square-root curve, not a constant-ratio
   curve.** $J_i / \sqrt{i}$ stays in a narrow band (62.9 to 75.6)
   across $i = 10$ to $1000$, while $J_i / i$ falls monotonically from
   70 to 2.391. The value 2.391 at $i = 1000$ is the crossing point of
   a falling cap-controlled ratio, not an asymptotic constant
   (Section 1). Extrapolating "the extremal ratio is about 2.4" from
   the cap-10000 frontier would be numerology; the cap-20000 frontier
   is predicted to give $J_{1000} \approx 3194$, ratio 3.19, which
   will confirm or kill this reading cheaply.
3. **The proof-relevant tight locus is $n = 2j$, $j = ci$, not the cap
   frontier.** Closing $j \le ci$ for all $n$ by an EEES78-strength
   input requires an exponent $\theta_{\mathrm{req}}(c)$ rising from
   $1/2$ at $c = 3/2$ to $0.616$ at $c = 2$, $0.664$ at $c = 2.4$, and
   $0.706$ at $c = 3$ (Section 3, table). Equivalently the reduction
   inequality at $n = 2j$ is short by $S(c)$ nats per unit $i$, with
   $S(2) = 0.523$ and $S(2.4) = 0.804$. Finite-$i$ values at
   $i = 100$ and $i = 1000$ already match the asymptotic $S(c)$ to
   three decimals, so this table is a reliable design spec for any
   strengthening attempt, not an asymptotic hope.
4. **Cap-frontier tightness is a discretisation artifact.** The log
   gap between $C(10001, i)$ and the frontier value
   $C(2J_i, 2(J_i - i))$ is $O(1)$ nats (median 0.32, max 1.07) against
   thresholds whose logs run to about 3250 nats: relative tightness
   below $3 \times 10^{-4}$ everywhere. A handful of $i$ have
   near-zero gaps ($i = 225$ is tightest, gap $0.0005$ nats); these
   are consistent with random fractional-part accidents and carry no
   visible family structure (Section 1.3).

## 1. Frontier data analysis

### 1.1 Method and ranges

$J_i$ (maximal fully cleared $j$ at cap $N^* = 10000$) was read from
pairpass.json (max of each cleared list; total reproduced: 768,741)
and agrees with the RESULTS.md appendix. Tightness quantities were
computed in floating point with `lgamma` (log-gamma), for all
$i = 1$ to $1000$:

- slack: $\sigma_i = \log C(10001, i) - \log C(2J_i, 2(J_i - i)) \ge 0$
  (how far the last cleared pair sits below the threshold);
- overshoot: $\omega_i = \log C(2(J_i+1), 2(J_i+1-i)) - \log C(10001, i) > 0$
  (how far the first uncleared pair sits above it).

Exact-integer spot checks: $N(1, j)$ and $N(2, j)$ were verified
against brute-force $N(i,j)$ for $j$ up to 30 (Section 2.3), and the
boundary values $N(1,70) = 9729 \le 10000 < 10010 = N(1,71)$ and
$N(2,93) = 9826 \le 10000 < 10040 = N(2,94)$ reproduce $J_1 = 70$ and
$J_2 = 93$ exactly. Total computation: two short Python jobs, under
5 seconds combined.

### 1.2 Shape of the frontier: $J_i \approx K\sqrt{i}$, $K$ slowly rising

| $i$ | $J_i$ | $J_i/i$ | $J_i/\sqrt{i}$ |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 70 | 70.00 | 70.0 |
| 2 | 93 | 46.50 | 65.8 |
| 10 | 199 | 19.90 | 62.9 |
| 50 | 454 | 9.08 | 64.2 |
| 100 | 656 | 6.56 | 65.6 |
| 200 | 955 | 4.78 | 67.5 |
| 300 | 1196 | 3.99 | 69.1 |
| 500 | 1597 | 3.19 | 71.4 |
| 700 | 1939 | 2.77 | 73.3 |
| 1000 | 2391 | 2.39 | 75.6 |

The frontier is defined implicitly by
$\log C(N^*+1, i) = \log C(2J_i, 2(J_i-i))$, an entropy equation
$N^* H(i/N^*) \approx 2J_i H(i/J_i)$ with $H$ the binary entropy
(natural log). In the regime $1 \ll i \ll N^*$ with $J_i \gg i$,
Stirling gives the leading law $J_i \sim \sqrt{N^* i / e}$, i.e.
$J_i/\sqrt{i} \to \sqrt{N^*/e} = 60.65$ at $N^* = 10000$. The observed
$K_i = J_i/\sqrt{i}$ sits above 60.65 and rises slowly (62.9 at
$i = 10$ to 75.6 at $i = 1000$) because neither small-parameter
approximation is clean at the top of the range: at $i = 1000$,
$i/N^* = 0.1$ and $i/J_i = 0.42$, and the true entropy terms exceed
their small-ratio expansions, pushing $J_i$ up. Both effects are
quantitative, not mysterious: solving the entropy equation at
$i = 1000$ returns $J \approx 2391$ on the nose.

Increments $\Delta_i = J_{i+1} - J_i$ confirm the concave
square-root shape: mean 7.84 for $i$ in $[1, 50)$, 4.04 in $[50, 100)$,
2.70 in $[100, 300)$, 1.92 in $[300, 600)$, 1.55 in $[600, 1000)$,
with the last ten increments alternating 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1.
A constant-ratio frontier $J_i \approx ci$ would have increments
growing linearly (about $c$ per step at every scale, ratio
$J_i/i$ constant); the data show the opposite signature on every
window. **Conclusion: the number 2.4 in the brief is
$\sqrt{N^*/e}$-type cap geometry evaluated at $i = 1000$, not an
extremal ratio of the problem.** Any strategy premised on "the
extremal families live near $j \approx 2.4i$" is premised on an
artifact. What the frontier does tell us is the cost curve of the
sweep method itself: cleared pairs per unit $i$ shrink like
$\sqrt{i}$, and doubling the cap multiplies $J_i$ by about $\sqrt{2}$
at small $i$ and by about $1.34$ at $i = 1000$ (Section 5).

### 1.3 Tightness at the frontier

Summary over $i = 1$ to $1000$: slack $\sigma_i$ has min 0.0005,
median 0.32, max 1.07 nats; overshoot $\omega_i$ has min 0.0004,
median 0.31, max 1.04 nats. Their sum $\sigma_i + \omega_i$ is the
log-step of $B_j = C(2j, 2(j-i))$ per unit $j$ at the frontier; it
grows from 0.028 at $i = 1$ to about 1.08 at $i = 1000$, tracking
$\partial_j \log B_j \approx 2\log(J_i/(J_i - i))$-type growth. Three
consequences:

- **Every frontier pair is "barely cleared" in absolute log terms**,
  trivially: the threshold $\log C(10001, i)$ (3251 nats at
  $i = 1000$) falls inside one $B_j$ step of size at most about
  1.1 nats, so the relative gap is at most $3 \times 10^{-4}$. This
  is what a fine discretisation of a smooth curve looks like. It is
  not evidence that the reduction is "nearly tight" along the
  frontier in any proof-relevant sense; tightness for a proof is
  measured at fixed ratio $c = j/i$ as $i \to \infty$ (Section 3),
  where the shortfall is $S(c) \cdot i$, extensive in $i$, not $O(1)$.
- **Anomalously tight $i$ exist but look accidental.** Smallest
  slacks: $i = 225$ ($\sigma = 0.00048$; $C(10001, 225)$ exceeds
  $C(2J, 2(J-i))$ by a factor $e^{0.00048} \approx 1.0005$),
  $i = 141$ (0.0033), $i = 883$ (0.0036), $i = 70$ (0.0040),
  $i = 827$ (0.0044), $i = 149$ (0.0061), $i = 99$ (0.0065),
  $i = 793$ (0.0066). Smallest overshoots: $i = 8$ (0.00041),
  $i = 1$ (0.0010), $i = 124$ (0.0012), $i = 468$ (0.0015),
  $i = 26$ (0.0015). With 1000 thresholds landing in steps of size
  up to about 1 nat, a minimum gap of order $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$ is
  the expected extreme-value behaviour of roughly uniform fractional
  parts; the tight $i$ values (225, 141, 883, 70, 827, ...) show no
  arithmetic pattern (not prime, not smooth, not a recurrence), and
  the tight sets for slack and overshoot are disjoint in character.
  Verdict: Diophantine accidents, useful only as regression tests for
  future classifiers (a cap-20000 classifier that misplaces $i = 225$
  by one $j$ has a bug, since its margin is the thinnest).
- **Largest slacks** cluster at the top ($i = 982$: 1.067; $i = 975$:
  1.056; $i = 989$: 1.051), simply because the $B_j$ step is largest
  there. Also an artifact.

### 1.4 What the frontier is actually good for

(i) Calibrating sweep cost and predicting cap-20000 output
(Section 5, with exact predictions for $i = 1, 2$). (ii) Regression
tests via the thinnest-margin $i$. (iii) A negative result for
strategy design: no extremal family is visible in the cap frontier,
because the cap frontier is the level set of a cap-dependent entropy
equation. The extremal locus for proof purposes must be derived
analytically (Section 3), and there it is the ray $j = ci$ at
$n = 2j$.

## 2. The cases $i = 1$ and $i = 2$: complete elementary closures

### 2.1 Why $i \le 2$ is special

The Phase 0 key lemma says: if $d \mid C(n,i)$ and
$\gcd(d, C(n,j)) = 1$, then $d \mid C(j, i)$, via the identity
$C(n,i)\,C(n-i, j-i) = C(n,j)\,C(j,i)$. The conjecture can therefore
fail only through the divisor $V_i(n)$ of $C(n,i)$, and EEES78 is
needed to show $V_i(n)$ is too large to fit inside $C(j,i)$. But for
$i = 1$ and $i = 2$ the set of primes $\ge i$ is the set of all
primes, so $V_i(n) = C(n,i)$ itself, and "too large to fit" is
immediate from $n > j$. EEES78 has no exceptions at $i \le 2$ (its
12 exception pairs all have $i \ge 3$), consistently.

### 2.2 Theorem ($i = 1$). For all $n$ and all $1 < j \le n/2$,
$\gcd(n, C(n,j)) \ge n / \gcd(n, j) \ge 2$. In particular some prime
divides both $C(n,1)$ and $C(n,j)$, and the $i = 1$ case of #699
holds for all $j$, $n$, with no exceptions.

*Proof.* Write $d = \gcd(n, j)$, $n = d n'$, $j = d j'$ with
$\gcd(n', j') = 1$. From $C(n,j) = \frac{n}{j} C(n-1, j-1)$ we get
$j' \cdot C(n,j) = n' \cdot C(n-1, j-1)$. Since $\gcd(j', n') = 1$,
$j' \mid C(n-1, j-1)$ (Euclid), say $C(n-1, j-1) = j' t$; then
$C(n,j) = n' t$. Hence $n' \mid C(n,j)$, and also $n' \mid n$, so
$n' \mid \gcd(n, C(n,j))$. Finally $d = \gcd(n,j) \le j \le n/2$, so
$n' = n/d \ge 2$. Any prime divisor of $n'$ is a prime $\ge 1$
dividing both binomials. $\square$

Remarks. (a) The brief asked for a Kummer analysis of when
$\gcd(n, C(n,j)) = 1$ can happen: the answer is **never** (in the
range $j \le n/2$; indeed the divisibility $n/\gcd(n,j) \mid C(n,j)$
holds for every $1 \le j < n$). The obstruction family is empty.
The families the brief singled out are all subsumed: $n = p$ prime
($n' = p \mid C(n,j)$, the classical fact), $n = p^a$ ($n' = p^{a - v_p(j)} \ge p$
unless $j$ absorbs all of $p^a$, impossible since $j \le n/2$),
$n$ with several prime factors (same argument prime by prime is not
even needed). (b) Kummer's theorem is consistent but strictly weaker
as a route here: it would analyse carries prime by prime, while the
identity disposes of all primes at once and even identifies a
divisor, not just a prime. (c) Verified computationally:
$n/\gcd(n,j) \mid C(n,j)$ for all $n < 120$, all $j \le n/2$, zero
failures. (d) Note $V_1(n) = n$, so this is exactly the conjecture
at $i = 1$, proved without the reduction: $N(1,j)$ is never needed.

### 2.3 Theorem ($i = 2$). For all $n$ and all $2 < j \le n/2$,
$\gcd(C(n,2), C(n,j)) > 1$. Hence the $i = 2$ case of #699 holds
for all $j$, $n$, with no exceptions (any prime divisor of the gcd
is $\ge 2 = i$).

*Proof.* $V_2(n) = C(n,2)$ since every prime is $\ge 2$. Suppose
$\gcd(C(n,2), C(n,j)) = 1$. The identity with $i = 2$,
$C(n,2)\,C(n-2, j-2) = C(n,j)\,C(j,2)$, gives
$C(n,2) \mid C(n,j)\,C(j,2)$, and Euclid's lemma with the assumed
coprimality gives $C(n,2) \mid C(j,2)$. But $n > j \ge 3$ implies
$C(n,2) > C(j,2) > 0$, contradiction. $\square$

Verified computationally: $\gcd(C(n,2), C(n,j)) > 1$ for all
$n < 120$, $3 \le j \le n/2$, zero failures. (In fact the gcd is
usually large; e.g. it equals 66 at $(n, j) = (12, 5)$.)

Both theorems are the Phase 0 lemma with $d = C(n,i)$ in place of
$d = V_i(n)$; they work precisely where $V_i = C(n,i)$. This
pinpoints the real obstruction for $i \ge 3$: a counterexample
forces $V_i(n) \mid C(j, j-i)$ (Phase 0), so the whole game is
whether the small-prime part $U_i(n) = C(n,i)/V_i(n)$ can be large
enough, often enough, to let $V_i(n)$ fit under $C(j, j-i)$ while
$n$ is large. EEES78 ($U_i < V_i$ outside 12 pairs) is one blunt
instrument for exactly this; Section 3 prices finer ones.

### 2.4 The exact thresholds $N(1, j)$ and $N(2, j)$ (as assigned)

$N(1, j)$: $C(n, 1) = n < C(2j, 2) = j(2j-1)$ iff
$n \le j(2j-1) - 1$. So $N(1, j) = j(2j-1) - 1 = 2j^2 - j - 1$,
as conjectured in the brief; verified against brute force for
$j = 2$ to $30$ and at the cap boundary ($N(1,70) = 9729$,
$N(1,71) = 10010$).

$N(2, j)$: $C(n, 2) < C(2j, 4)$ iff
$n(n-1) < \frac{(2j)(2j-1)(2j-2)(2j-3)}{12}$. Solving the quadratic
in $n$ exactly,
$$
N(2, j) = \left\lceil \tfrac{1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{(2j)(2j-1)(2j-2)(2j-3)}{3}}}{2} \right\rceil - 1
= \max\{ n : n(n-1) < \tfrac{(2j)(2j-1)(2j-2)(2j-3)}{12} \}.
$$
Verified against brute force for $j = 3$ to $30$. Asymptotically
$N(2, j) \sim \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} j^2 \approx 1.1547\, j^2$
(e.g. $N(2, 100) = 11374$ vs $11547$ asymptotic). Boundary checks:
$N(2, 93) = 9826$, $N(2, 94) = 10040$, reproducing $J_2 = 93$;
$N(2, 132) = 19891 \le 20000 < 20195 = N(2, 133)$, predicting
$J_2 = 132$ at cap 20000 exactly. Similarly $N(1, 100) = 19899$,
$N(1, 101) = 20300$ predict $J_1 = 100$ at cap 20000 exactly.

These formulas are now of calibration value only: after Sections
2.2 and 2.3, no proof for $i \le 2$ passes through $N(i, j)$ at all.

## 3. The $c$ versus required-strengthening table

### 3.1 Setup

Fix a ratio $c > 3/2$ and ask what input would close all pairs with
$j \le ci$ for all $n \ge 2j$, in the shape of the existing proof.
Generalise EEES78 to an exponent: suppose one knew
$V_i(n) > C(n,i)^{\theta}$ outside finitely many exceptions. A
counterexample forces $V_i(n) \mid C(j, j-i)$ (Phase 0), hence
$C(n,i)^{\theta} < C(j, j-i)$, and since $C(n,i) \ge C(2j, i)$ it
suffices to have $C(2j, i)^{\theta} \ge C(j, j-i)$ along $j = ci$.
The binding case is $n = 2j$: larger $n$ only enlarges $C(n,i)$.
Note also that the brief's suggested diagnostic,
$M(c) = \lim \frac{1}{i}\left[\log C(2j, 2(j-i)) - \log C(2j, i)\right]$
at $j = ci$, is exactly the per-unit-$i$ log shortfall of the
current ($\theta = 1/2$) reduction at $n = 2j$, because
$\log C(2j, 2(j-i))$ and $2 \log C(j, j-i)$ agree to leading order
(Section 3.3). Both formalisations give the same number:

$$
S(c) \;=\; 2c\left[ H\!\left(\tfrac{1}{c}\right) - H\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2c}\right) \right]
\quad\text{nats per unit } i,
\qquad
\theta_{\mathrm{req}}(c) \;=\; \frac{H(1/c)}{2\,H(1/(2c))},
$$
with $H(p) = -p \log p - (1-p)\log(1-p)$. Indeed
$\frac{1}{i}\log C(ci, (c-1)i) \to cH(1/c)$ and
$\frac{1}{i}\log C(2ci, i) \to 2cH(1/(2c))$, so
$\theta_{\mathrm{req}}$ is the limiting ratio
$\log C(j, j-i) / \log C(2j, i)$, and $S(c)$ is the limiting
$\frac{1}{i}[\log C(2j, 2(j-i)) - \log C(2j, i)]$. Checks:
$S(3/2) = 0$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{req}}(3/2) = 1/2$ exactly
($H(2/3) = H(1/3)$), recovering the known closure point;
$\theta_{\mathrm{req}}(c) \to 1$ as $c \to \infty$, as it must:
for very large $j/i$ one would need essentially all of $C(n,i)$ to
sit on large primes.

Reading: to close $j \le 2i$ by this route one needs an input of
EEES78 type with exponent $0.616$ in place of $1/2$, uniformly
outside finitely many exceptions; equivalently one needs to beat
the current comparison $C(n,i) < C(2j, 2(j-i))$ by a factor
$e^{0.523\, i}$ at $n = 2j$, $j = 2i$. At $c = 2.4$ (the ratio the
cap frontier happens to display at $i = 1000$) the price is
exponent $0.664$, or $e^{0.804\, i}$. These are large, structured
demands: EEES78's $u < v$ is a theorem about the *typical* split of
$C(n,i)$, and an exponent above $1/2$ fails in general (the 12
exceptions already fail at $1/2$; for any fixed $\theta > 1/2$ one
expects, and in small cases can exhibit, infinitely many $(n, i)$
with $V_i(n) \le C(n,i)^{\theta}$, e.g. $i$ fixed and $n$ ranging
over multiples of high powers of small primes). So the table should
be read as a **lower bound on what a pure size argument must
deliver, and therefore as evidence that closing $c > 3/2$ needs a
different input type**: not a better global exponent, but
information that couples $(n, i)$ to $j$ (for instance, that the
same primes witnessing largeness of $V_i(n)$ are constrained modulo
$C(j, j-i)$, or $p$-adic lower bounds for a *specific* prime
$p \ge i$ dividing $C(n,i)$ to also divide $C(n,j)$, Kummer-style,
rather than a size comparison of the whole $V_i(n)$).

### 3.3 Two supporting computations

- **Finite-$i$ confirmation.** The exact finite quantity
  $\frac{1}{i}[\log C(2j, 2(j-i)) - \log C(2j, i)]$ at $n = 2j$:
  $c = 2$: 0.5218 at $i = 100$, 0.5231 at $i = 1000$ (limit 0.5232);
  $c = 2.4$: 0.8018 at $i = 100$, 0.8036 at $i = 1000$ (limit
  0.8038); $c = 3$: 1.1155 at $i = 1000$ (limit 1.1157); $c = 3/2$:
  0.0000 at $i = 500$ and $i = 1000$ (exactly 0, since
  $C(2j, 2(j-i)) = C(2j, i)$ when $2(j-i) = i$). Convergence is fast;
  the table is usable at proof-design scale already for $i \ge 100$.
- **The Vandermonde step is not where the loss is.** The current
  proof replaces $C(j, j-i)^2$ by $C(2j, 2(j-i))$. The log loss
  $\log C(2j, 2(j-i)) - 2\log C(j, j-i)$ is 2.53 nats at
  $(i, j) = (100, 200)$, 3.68 at $(1000, 2000)$, 3.76 at
  $(1000, 2400)$: $O(\log i)$, a polynomial factor, negligible
  against the $S(c) \cdot i$ shortfall. Sharpening Vandermonde buys
  nothing exponential. Likewise the squaring $v^2 > C(n,i)$ is
  exactly the $\theta = 1/2$ input; all exponential loss sits in
  the gap between the true typical size of $V_i(n)$ and
  $C(n,i)^{1/2}$, localised at $n = 2j$.

## 4. Honest assessment: proof ingredients versus numerology

**Proof ingredients (proved here, checkable line by line):**
the $i = 1$ theorem with the explicit divisor $n/\gcd(n,j)$; the
$i = 2$ theorem via the identity lemma; the exact $N(1,j)$ and
$N(2,j)$ formulas with brute-force verification ranges stated; the
$S(c)$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{req}}(c)$ formulas, derived from
Stirling with finite-$i$ confirmation to three decimals. The
$i \le 2$ closures also retroactively explain two data points: the
sweep's cleared lists for $i = 1, 2$ were guaranteed in full (the
pairs exist for all $j$; only the cap truncated them), and EEES78's
exception list avoiding $i \le 2$ is forced, not a coincidence.

**Solid data analysis (computation, no proof content):** the
$J_i \approx K\sqrt{i}$ frontier shape and its entropy explanation;
increments; tightness statistics. These describe the *method's*
reach, not the problem's extremal structure. Their proof value is
indirect: cost calibration, classifier regression tests, and the
negative guidance of Section 1.4.

**Numerology, flagged as such:** the ratio $J_{1000}/1000 = 2.391$
and any "extremal ratio near 2.4" narrative (cap artifact, Section
1.2); the anomalously tight $i$ list (225, 141, 883, ...) as
anything but fractional-part accidents and test cases; the slow
rise of $K_i = J_i/\sqrt{i}$ from 62.9 to 75.6 as anything but
quantifiable entropy corrections. Nothing in Sections 1.2 and 1.3
should be cited as evidence about where counterexamples or extremal
families for #699 itself would live; if a counterexample exists it
sits at some $(i, j)$ with $N(i,j)$ astronomically large or $i$
large, a regime no cap frontier samples.

**The one genuinely strategic number** is $\theta_{\mathrm{req}}(c)$:
it converts "extend the closure" into a priced target and shows
the price is a *qualitative* jump (past any plausible uniform
exponent theorem), redirecting effort toward prime-specific
($p$-adic/Kummer) or coupling arguments, and toward the $n = 2j$
endpoint, where the deficit is attained and where Bloom's page
already asserts (without readable proof) a separate closure.

   $J_{1000} \approx 3194$ (entropy/binary-search in floats, $\pm 1$).
   If $J_{1000}(20000)/1000 \approx 3.19$, the cap-artifact reading
   of "2.4" is confirmed experimentally; if it instead stays near
   2.4, Section 1.2 is wrong and the frontier has a ratio regime
   this analysis missed, which would itself be a discovery.
2. **Where the $i \le 2$ method breaks:** exactly at $i = 3$, where
   the prime 2 leaves $V_i$. The first genuinely open local question
   this strategy poses: for $i = 3$, $V_3(n) = C(n,3)$ with all
   factors 2 removed; a counterexample needs $V_3(n) \mid C(j, j-3)$
   *and* the full 2-part of $C(n,3)$ to be coprime to $C(n,j)$ in
   the sense that no odd prime of $C(n,3)$ divides $C(n,j)$ either.
   A $p$-adic analysis at $p = 2$ alone cannot close $i = 3$, but
   quantifying how often $v_2(C(n,3))$ is large ( $n \equiv 0, 1$
   mod high powers of 2) identifies the thin set of $n$ where
   $i = 3$ is even capable of failing the size test; that set, not
   the cap frontier, is the right "frontier" for the next strategy
   round.
3. **Where the $S(c)$ pricing breaks:** it prices only
   EEES78-shaped inputs (a uniform exponent). It says nothing
   against arguments using the multiplicative structure of
   $C(j, j-i)$ (e.g. a prime $p \in [i, j)$ dividing $V_i(n)$ to a
   higher power than $C(j, j-i)$ can absorb), nor against
   $n = 2j$-specific arguments. A strategy that beats the table
   must state which of these it exploits; one that does not is
   implicitly claiming a uniform exponent $\theta > 1/2$ theorem
   and should be refereed against that claim.
4. **Data that would sharpen the picture:** (a) the cap-20000 $J_i$
   list (tests Section 1.2 and the thinnest-margin regression cases,
   notably $i = 225$); (b) for moderate $i$ (say $3 \le i \le 30$),
   the *actual* maximal ratio $\log V_i(n) / \log C(n,i)$ over
   $n \le N(i, j)$ along rays $j = ci$: this measures how far the
   true $V_i$ sits above the $C(n,i)^{1/2}$ floor near the binding
   locus $n = 2j$, and whether the binding constraint in practice
   is the exponent or the divisibility $V_i \mid C(j, j-i)$;
   (c) the distribution of $\gcd(V_i(n), C(n,j))$ itself (not just
   $> 1$ versus $= 1$) on swept triples: if the gcd is typically
   enormous, size arguments are leaving structure on the table; if
   it is often a single small prime just above $i$, prime-specific
   arguments are the natural next step. None of (a) to (c) requires
   new sweep machinery, only new reductions of existing or imminent
   outputs.
