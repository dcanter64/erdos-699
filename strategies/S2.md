# Strategy S2 — Squeeze the Vandermonde step

**Route:** extract everything the Vandermonde identity actually gives in the
Phase 0 chain, instead of the single central term, and measure exactly how
much of the open region $j>3i/2$ that buys.
**Verdict, up front:** this is **not a standalone route**. The Vandermonde
gain is a polynomial factor $G(i,j)\asymp i^{1/2}$ sitting next to an
exponential gap $\asymp e^{\Theta(i)}$. Asymptotically it contributes
*nothing* to the closure threshold: the exponent of strengthened EEES-type
input needed at $j=ci$ is the same with or without the squeeze. It is a
small, free, finite-size amplifier (worth using in the *computational*
definition of $N(i,j)$, and it shaves a few percent off the required
strengthened exponent at $i\approx 100$, decaying like $(\log i)/i$), and,
paired with a hypothetical strengthened input $v^{2}\ge \binom ni^{1+\varepsilon}$,
it is the bookkeeping that converts such an $\varepsilon$ into an explicit
$c(\varepsilon)>3/2$. All numbers below were computed in Python stdlib
(`math.lgamma`, exact log-binomials); runtime seconds.

Notation: $1\le i<j\le n/2$, $d=j-i$, $v=V_i(n)$, $C(a,b)=\binom ab$.
A counterexample means $\gcd(v,C(n,j))=1$ and $(n,i)$ not an EEES78
exception. Phase 0 gives, for such a triple,

$$v\mid C(j,d),\qquad v^{2}>C(n,i)\ \ (\text{EEES78}),\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
C(n,i)<C(j,d)^{2}<C(2j,2d)=:C(2j,2(j-i)). \tag{$\ast$}$$

The last inequality is Vandermonde: $C(2j,2d)=\sum_k C(j,k)C(j,2d-k)$,
a sum of positive terms one of which ($k=d$) is $C(j,d)^2$.
Define the gain factor

$$G(i,j)=\frac{C(2j,2d)}{C(j,d)^{2}} .$$

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

Phase 0 closes $j\le 3i/2$ by $C(2j,2d)\le C(2j,i)\le C(n,i)$, contradicting
$(\ast)$. For $j>3i/2$ the first of those inequalities reverses, and the
question is whether the slack thrown away in $(\ast)$ — the factor $G(i,j)$,
i.e. the whole Vandermonde sum rather than its central term — can pay for
the reversal. It cannot, for a clean entropy reason: at $j=ci$ the two
sides $C(2j,2d)$ and $C(j,d)^2$ have *identical* exponential growth rates
(the entropy terms cancel exactly, Lemma 2), so $G$ is only
$\sqrt{\pi i(c-1)/c}\,(1+o(1))$, whereas the reversal factor
$R(i,j)=C(2j,2d)/C(2j,i)$ grows like $e^{\kappa(c)i}$ with
$\kappa(c)>0$ for every $c>3/2$ (Lemma 4). Consequently *no argument using
only the two size facts $v\le C(j,d)$ and $v^2>C(n,i)$ can close any fixed
$c>3/2$* — those two facts are simultaneously satisfiable at $n=2j$
(Lemma 5, a size-only impossibility statement). Closing $j\le ci$ requires
genuinely new input about $v$, quantified exactly in §5: a strengthened
EEES bound $v^2\ge C(n,i)^{1+\varepsilon}$ with
$\varepsilon>\mathrm{need}(c)=H(1/c)/H(1/(2c))-1$ (e.g. $\varepsilon>0.065$
for $c=1.6$, $\varepsilon>0.233$ for $c=2$), and no such uniform
$\varepsilon>0$ is currently known.

## 2. Term ratios and truncated Vandermonde bounds

**Lemma 1 (exact term ratios).** Let $T_k=C(j,k)C(j,2d-k)$, so
$C(2j,2d)=\sum_{k=0}^{2d}T_k$, $T_d=C(j,d)^2$, and $T_{d+t}=T_{d-t}$.
For $0\le t<d$,

$$\frac{T_{d+t+1}}{T_{d+t}}=\rho_t:=
\frac{(j-d-t)\,(d-t)}{(d+t+1)\,(j-d+t+1)},\qquad 0<\rho_t<1,$$

and $\rho_t$ is strictly decreasing in $t$. Hence
$T_{d+t}/T_d=\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}\rho_s$.

*Proof sketch.* $C(j,m+1)/C(j,m)=(j-m)/(m+1)$ applied to the first factor
with $m=d+t$ and to the second factor with $m=d-t-1$ (note
$C(j,d-t-1)/C(j,d-t)=(d-t)/(j-d+t+1)$) gives the formula; each of the four
factors moves the wrong way in $t$, giving monotonicity; $\rho_t<1$ is
$(j-d-t)(d-t)<(d+t+1)(j-d+t+1)$, immediate since $j-d-t\le j-d+t+1$ and
$d-t\le d+t+1$ with at least one strict. Symmetry $T_{d+t}=T_{d-t}$ is
$k\leftrightarrow 2d-k$. $\square$

**Corollary 1.1 (explicit finite truncations).**
With $r_t=T_{d+t}/T_d$,

$$G(i,j)\ \ge\ 1+2r_1\ =\ 1+\frac{2d\,(j-d)}{(d+1)(j-d+1)},\qquad
G(i,j)\ \ge\ 1+2r_1+2r_1\rho_1,\ \ \text{etc.}$$

For fixed $c=j/i\in(3/2,2]$ and $i\to\infty$:
$r_1=1-\frac{c}{(c-1)i}+O(i^{-2})$, so the three-term truncation gives
only $G\ge 3-o(1)$; indeed *any fixed* number $2m+1$ of central terms gives
$G\ge (2m+1)-o(1)$, because $r_t\to1$ for each fixed $t$. Truncation at
fixed width therefore never sees the true size of $G$: the mass is spread
over $\asymp\sqrt i$ terms (Lemma 2, remark). Numerically at
$(i,j)=(100,160)$ ($c=1.6$): $r_1=0.9739$, $r_2=0.8995$, $r_3=0.7879$;
three-term bound $2.948$, five-term bound $4.747$, true $G=10.882$.

*Use.* Corollary 1.1 is a clean, fully explicit $G\ge1+\delta(i,j)$ with
$\delta(i,j)=2d(j-d)/((d+1)(j-d+1))$ — e.g. $\delta\ge1$ as soon as
$d(j-d)\ge (d+1)(j-d+1)/2$, which holds for all but small $(i,j)$ in the
open region. It triples the Phase 0 bound for free. It is also, by itself,
worthless asymptotically, for the reason just given.

## 3. Exact asymptotics of $\log G$: the entropy cancellation

**Lemma 2.** Fix $c\in(3/2,2]$, let $j=ci+O(1)$, $d=(c-1)i+O(1)$. Then

$$\log G(i,j)=\tfrac12\log i+\tfrac12\log\frac{\pi(c-1)}{c}+o(1),
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
G(i,j)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi i\,(c-1)}{c}}\;\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).$$

In particular $\log G/\log C(j,d)\to0$: the gain is invisible at
exponential scale.

*Proof sketch (Stirling, carefully).* Use
$\log C(N,K)=NH(p)-\tfrac12\log(2\pi Np(1-p))+O(1/N)$ with
$p=K/N$, $H(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)$ (natural log).
For $C(2j,2d)$: $N=2j$, $p=2d/2j=d/j=(c-1)/c$, giving
$2j\,H\!\bigl(\frac{c-1}{c}\bigr)-\tfrac12\log\bigl(4\pi j\,p(1-p)\bigr)+O(1/i)$.
For $C(j,d)$: $N=j$, *the same* $p=d/j$, giving
$j\,H\!\bigl(\frac{c-1}{c}\bigr)-\tfrac12\log\bigl(2\pi j\,p(1-p)\bigr)+O(1/i)$.
Doubling the second and subtracting from the first, the entropy terms
$2jH(p)$ cancel **exactly** — this is the whole story — leaving
$\log G=-\tfrac12\log\bigl(4\pi jp(1-p)\bigr)+\log\bigl(2\pi jp(1-p)\bigr)+o(1)
=\tfrac12\log\bigl(\pi j p(1-p)\bigr)+o(1)$.
With $p(1-p)=\frac{c-1}{c}\cdot\frac1c$ and $j=ci$:
$\pi j p(1-p)=\pi i(c-1)/c$. $\square$

*Remark (why $\sqrt i$ terms: local limit).* $T_{d+t}/C(2j,2d)$ is the
hypergeometric distribution of a sample of $2d$ draws from $2j$ items with
$j$ successes, evaluated at its mean $d$; its variance is
$\sigma^2=\frac{d\,(j-d)}{2j}(1+o(1))=\frac{i(c-1)}{2c}(1+o(1))$, and
$G=1/\Pr[X=d]\approx\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$, matching Lemma 2. A truncation
capturing a constant fraction of $G$ must keep $|t|\lesssim\sigma\asymp\sqrt i$
terms, with $r_t\approx e^{-t^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ from Lemma 1's product.
(Direct expansion of $\log\rho_s$ gives the same Gaussian; we record this
as a sketch, not a proved bound with explicit constants — see §6.)

## 4. The real comparison, and the exact amount of extra factor needed

Phase 0's closing step needs $C(2j,2d)$ (or, sharply, $C(j,d)^2$) to be
$\le C(n,i)$; since $n\ge2j$ the hardest case is $n=2j$. Define the
reversal factor and the task's $\mathrm{need}$ quantity, at $n=2j$:

$$R(i,j)=\frac{C(2j,2d)}{C(2j,i)},\qquad
\mathrm{need}(i,j)=\frac{\log C(2j,2d)-\log C(2j,i)}{\log C(2j,i)}
=\frac{\log R(i,j)}{\log C(2j,i)}.$$

**Lemma 3 (limit of $\mathrm{need}$).** For $j=ci+O(1)$, $c\in(3/2,2]$,

$$\mathrm{need}(i,j)\longrightarrow \mathrm{need}(c):=
\frac{H(1/c)-H\!\bigl(1/(2c)\bigr)}{H\!\bigl(1/(2c)\bigr)}
=\frac{H(1/c)}{H(1/(2c))}-1>0,
\qquad \log R(i,j)=2ci\bigl[H(1/c)-H(1/(2c))\bigr]+O(\log i).$$

*Proof sketch.* $\log C(2j,2d)=2jH(d/j)+O(\log i)=2ciH(1/c)+O(\log i)$,
using $H(p)=H(1-p)$ so $H((c-1)/c)=H(1/c)$. And $\log C(2j,i)$ has
$N=2j$, $K=i$, $p=1/(2c)$: $=2ciH(1/(2c))+O(\log i)$. Divide. Positivity:
$H$ is strictly increasing on $(0,1/2]$ and $1/(2c)<1/c\le2/3$; for
$c\le2$, $1/c\in[1/2,2/3)$, $H(1/c)=H(1-1/c)$ with $1-1/c\in(1/3,1/2]$,
still $>H(1/(2c))$ since $1-1/c>1/(2c)\iff c>3/2$. Note the mechanism:
$\mathrm{need}(c)\to0$ exactly as $c\downarrow3/2$, recovering Phase 0's
threshold as the degenerate case. $\square$

| $c$ | 1.51 | 1.55 | 1.6 | 1.7 | 1.75 | 1.8 | 1.9 | 2.0 | 2.5 | 3.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{need}(c)$ | 0.0072 | 0.0343 | 0.0652 | 0.1184 | 0.1415 | 0.1627 | 0.2003 | 0.2326 | 0.3449 | 0.4127 |
| $\kappa(c)=2c[H(1/c)-H(1/(2c))]$ ($\log R\approx\kappa i$) | 0.0138 | 0.0669 | 0.1295 | 0.2438 | — | 0.3460 | 0.4386 | 0.5232 | 0.8630 | 1.1157 | Finite-$i$ values
track the limit closely: at $c=1.6$, $\mathrm{need}=0.0660$ ($i=100$),
$0.0653$ ($i=1000$); at $c=2$, $0.2352$, $0.2330$. But $\log R$ itself is
already $12.91$ at $(100,160)$ ($R\approx4.0\times10^{5}$) and $129.5$ at
$(1000,1600)$ ($R\approx1.7\times10^{56}$). Against this, $\log G$ is
$2.39$ and $3.54$. **The squeeze recovers under $3\%$ of the log-gap at
$i=1000$, $c=1.6$, and a vanishing fraction as $i\to\infty$.**

## 5. The conditional closure statement, exactly

**Lemma 4 (what strengthened input closes which $c$).** Suppose a
strengthened EEES-type bound holds outside finitely many exceptions:

$$v^{2}\ \ge\ C(n,i)^{\,1+\varepsilon}\qquad(\varepsilon>0\ \text{fixed}). \tag{EEES$_\varepsilon$}$$

Then for every fixed $c$ with $\mathrm{need}(c)<\varepsilon$, all
counterexamples with $3i/2<j\le ci$ are excluded for all sufficiently
large $i$ (with an effective threshold computable from Lemmas 2–3, the
remaining finite range being a computation of the existing type).
Conversely, if $\varepsilon<\mathrm{need}(c)$, (EEES$_\varepsilon$) plus
$(\ast)$ does not exclude $n=2j$ at $j\approx ci$ for large $i$.

*Proof sketch.* A counterexample gives, sharply (§6.1),
$C(n,i)^{1+\varepsilon}\le v^2\le C(j,d)^2$, i.e.
$C(n,i)^{1+\varepsilon}<C(2j,2d)$ a fortiori. Since $n\ge2j$,
$C(2j,i)^{1+\varepsilon}\le C(n,i)^{1+\varepsilon}$, so it suffices that
$C(2j,i)^{1+\varepsilon}\ge C(2j,2d)$, i.e.
$\varepsilon\ge\mathrm{need}(i,j)\to\mathrm{need}(c)$. If
$\varepsilon<\mathrm{need}(c)$, at $n=2j$,
$C(n,i)^{1+\varepsilon}<C(2j,2d)$ for large $i$, so the two displayed
inequalities are consistent — no contradiction is derivable from them.
Using the sharp middle term $C(j,d)^2$ instead of $C(2j,2d)$ replaces
$\mathrm{need}$ by $\mathrm{need}-\log G/\log C(2j,i)$; the correction is
$0.0122$ at $(100,160)$, $0.0018$ at $(1000,1600)$, and $\to0$. The
squeeze changes the required $\varepsilon$ only in the third significant
figure, and only for small $i$. $\square$

Equivalently, in the form $v\ge C(n,i)^{1/2+\eta}$: closure at $c$
needs $\eta>\mathrm{need}(c)/2$ — $\eta>0.0326$ at $c=1.6$, $\eta>0.116$
at $c=2$. Inverting: a given $\varepsilon$ closes up to the $c(\varepsilon)$
solving $H(1/c)=(1+\varepsilon)H(1/(2c))$; $c(\varepsilon)\downarrow3/2$
as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, e.g. $\varepsilon=0.01$ gives $c\approx1.52$,
$\varepsilon=0.03$ gives $c\approx1.545$.

**A form of "strengthened input" that cannot work.** A bound of the shape
$v\ge C(j,d)\,(1+\delta)$, $\delta>0$, is *impossible*, not merely
unavailable: the key lemma gives $v\mid C(j,d)$, hence $v\le C(j,d)$.
The only divisibility-side strengthening with content would be a lower
bound $v\ge C(j,d)^{1-\delta}$ or $v\ge C(j,d)/M$ with explicit small $M$;
combined with $v^2>C(n,i)$ this is exactly (EEES$_\varepsilon$) re-expressed
along the frontier $C(n,i)\approx C(j,d)^2$, since on that frontier
$\log v/\log C(j,d)$ and $\tfrac12\log C(n,i)/\log C(j,d)$ differ by the
$\mathrm{need}$ gap. There is no cheaper currency: any input closing $c$
must, on the frontier pairs, assert that $v$ exceeds the EEES78 floor
$C(n,i)^{1/2}$ by a factor $\ge R(i,j)^{1/2}=e^{\kappa(c)i/2+O(\log i)}$
($\approx 635$ at $(100,160)$; $\approx1.3\times10^{28}$ at $(1000,1600)$).

**Is (EEES$_\varepsilon$) available? No.** EEES78 is precisely
$\varepsilon=0$ with finitely many exceptions: it asserts $u<v$ for the
small-prime part $u=C(n,i)/v$, i.e. $v^2/C(n,i)=v/u>1$, with no uniform
margin. Nothing in the Phase 0 record bounds $v/u$ away from $1$
uniformly in $(n,i)$, and a uniform $\varepsilon>0$ would be a new theorem
of EEES type, not a refinement of the Vandermonde step. This route
therefore does not manufacture the input it needs; it only prices it.

## 6. Breaking points, honestly

**6.1 The sharp form of $(\ast)$ was already available, and it does not
help either.** The squaring in Phase 0 is not where the loss is: $v\le C(j,d)$
(unsquared) combined with $v>C(n,i)^{1/2}$ *is* $C(n,i)<C(j,d)^2$, by
squaring a true inequality of positive integers. Replacing
$C(2j,2d)$ by $C(j,d)^2$ throughout — i.e. defining
$N'(i,j)=\max\{n:C(n,i)<C(j,d)^2\}\le N(i,j)$ — is strictly sharper and
free, and we recommend it for future computations. Its effect: at fixed
$n$-cap, $N$ shrinks by $\Delta n\approx \log G/\log\frac{n}{\,n-i\,}$;
at $n=10000$, $i=1000$ this is $\approx 3.5/0.105\approx 33$ values of $n$
per pair — a few dozen extra cleared pairs' worth of boundary movement,
not a new region. (Estimate, not computed pair-by-pair.)

**6.2 Size-only impossibility beyond $3/2$ (the real breaking point).**

**Lemma 5.** Let $c>3/2$ and $i$ large, $j=ci+O(1)$, $n=2j$. Then the two
inequalities $v\le C(j,d)$ and $v^2>C(n,i)$ are simultaneously satisfiable
(e.g. by $v=C(j,d)$, as a *size* assignment), because
$C(j,d)^2>C(2j,i)\iff\log R(i,j)>\log G(i,j)$, and
$\log R-\log G=\kappa(c)i+O(\log i)\to+\infty$.

Hence any proof that uses $v$ only through (i) the divisibility bound
$v\le C(j,d)$ and (ii) the EEES78 size bound $v^2>C(n,i)$ — which is
exactly the Phase 0 toolkit, with or without the full Vandermonde sum —
**cannot** close any fixed $c>3/2$, for all large $i$. Closing the open
region requires using $v$ arithmetically (which primes, to which powers,
in which of $C(n,i),C(n,j)$), not just its magnitude. This is a statement
about the method, not about the conjecture: actual $v$'s are of course
not free variables.

**6.3 Where the explicit-constant version is unfinished.** Lemma 2 is an
asymptotic via Stirling with $O(1/N)$ terms uniform for fixed $c$; turning
it into "$G(i,j)\ge\sqrt{\pi i(c-1)/c}\,(1-1/i)$ for $i\ge i_0(c)$" with
explicit $i_0$ is routine but not done here. Likewise the Gaussian window
bound $r_t\ge e^{-t^2/\sigma^2}$-type estimate from Lemma 1's product
(expanding $\log\rho_s$ and bounding the tail) is sketched, not proved
with constants. Neither gap matters for the verdict: the *upper* bound
$G=O(\sqrt i)$, which is what kills the route, follows from Lemma 2's
computation with room to spare (even $G\le i$ for large $i$ suffices
against $e^{\kappa i}$).

**6.4 Small $i$ caveat.** For bounded $i$ the gain is a modest constant
($G=3.52$ at $(10,16)$) and $\mathrm{need}(i,j)$ is a finite computation;
nothing here precludes the squeeze, combined with the existing sweep
machinery, from clearing additional explicit finite ranges. It precludes
only an asymptotic closure.

## 7. Targeted partial-closure statement and conclusion

**Targeted statement (conditional, exact):** *This route closes
$j\le c\,i$ for every $c$ with $\mathrm{need}(c)
=H(1/c)/H(1/(2c))-1<\varepsilon$, provided the strengthened input
(EEES$_\varepsilon$): $v^2\ge C(n,i)^{1+\varepsilon}$ outside finitely many
exceptions. Unconditionally (with EEES78 as known, $\varepsilon=0$) it
closes no $c>3/2$ whatsoever: the Vandermonde gain $G(i,j)\asymp\sqrt i$
is asymptotically negligible against the reversal factor
$R(i,j)=e^{\kappa(c)i+O(\log i)}$, $\kappa(c)>0$.*

**Conclusion: amplifier, not a standalone route — and a weak amplifier.**
Quantitatively: at the representative frontier point $j=1.6i$ the full
Vandermonde sum buys a factor $G\approx10.9$ at $i=100$ and $34.3$ at
$i=1000$ ($\log G=2.39$, $3.54$) against a log-gap of $12.9$, resp.
$129.5$ — $18\%$, resp. $2.7\%$, of what is needed, with the fraction
$\to0$. Its genuine deliverables are: (i) the sharp inequality
$C(n,i)<C(j,d)^2$ replacing $C(2j,2d)$ in the definition of $N(i,j)$,
a free computational improvement worth $\Delta n\approx$ a few dozen at
the current sweep cap; (ii) the exact price list $\mathrm{need}(c)$
(§4–§5) that any future strengthened-EEES strategy (a different route)
must meet — $\varepsilon>0.065$ for $c=1.6$, $>0.142$ for $c=1.75$,
$>0.233$ for $c=2$ — together with Lemma 5's proof that *some* such
non-size input is necessary. Recommended pairing: discard S2 as a proof
engine; keep its $\mathrm{need}(c)$ table as the specification for
whichever route attacks the distribution of $v$ itself, and adopt the
$N'(i,j)$ sharpening in any future sweep.
