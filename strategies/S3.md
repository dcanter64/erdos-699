# Strategy S3 — Large prime factors and prime gaps

**Problem #699.** For $1 \le i < j \le n/2$, $\binom{n}{i}$ and $\binom{n}{j}$
always share a prime factor $\ge i$.
**Established inputs.** Phase 0 finite reduction: outside the 12 EEES78
exception pairs, a counterexample $(n,i,j)$ satisfies
$\binom{n}{i} < \binom{2j}{2(j-i)}$, hence $2j \le n \le N(i,j)$ where
$N(i,j) := \max\{n : \binom{n}{i} < \binom{2j}{2(j-i)}\}$; region
$j \le 3i/2$ closed; sweep: no counterexample with $n \le 10000$ in
$j > 3i/2$; $768{,}741$ pairs with $i \le 1000$ fully cleared, $3{,}480{,}759$
partially covered (RESULTS.md).

---

## 1. Idea

Look for the common prime divisor in the most forced place: the top of
$[1,n]$. A prime $p$ with $n/2 < p \le n$ can divide $\binom{n}{k}$
($k \le n/2$) only through a single uncancelled factor, and Legendre's
formula decides exactly when it does: $p \mid \binom{n}{j}$ iff
$p > n-j$, and $p \mid \binom{n}{i}$ iff $p > n-i$. So a prime in the
short interval $(n-i, n]$ divides **both** binomial coefficients and is
$\ge i$ automatically. A counterexample therefore forces $(n-i,n]$ to
be prime-free: $n$ must sit inside a prime gap of length at least $i+1$.
Prime gaps, however, are small and heavily controlled: explicit
theorems (Dusart, Trudgian, Axler) bound them by $n/\mathrm{polylog}(n)$
for all large $n$, and exhaustive tables bound them by $1476$ for all
$n \le 4\cdot 10^{18}$. Intersecting "gap $\ge i+1$ at $n$" with
"$n \le N(i,j)$" clears every pair whose $i$ exceeds the maximal gap
below $N(i,j)$ — an $i$-versus-$n$ closure, complementary to the
$j \le 3i/2$ closure ($j$ versus $i$) and to the sweep ($n \le 10000$
absolute). **Correction notice:** the brief's observation (c) is false
as stated (the interval inclusion is backwards); §2 proves the correct
version, gives a counterexample to the stated one, and traces the
consequence: every closure below is governed by $i$, not $j$. The route
is thereby weaker than the brief suggested, but it still fully clears
large explicit families beyond the sweep, including (Theorem C) every
open pair with $132 \le i \le 1000$ and $j \le 5000$ — i.e. all
partially covered pairs in that $i$-range — and every open pair with
$i \ge 1476$ and $j \le 7.35\cdot 10^{8}$. Its honest limit is equally
sharp: for fixed small $i$ (extremally $i=1$, where the route fires
only when $n$ itself is prime) it says almost nothing, and closing
$i=1$ for all $j$ by gaps alone would need uniform gaps
$O(n^{1/2})$, beyond even Baker–Harman–Pintz ($n^{0.525}$).

## 2. Exact lemmas

Legendre: $v_p\binom{n}{k} = \sum_{e\ge1}
\big(\lfloor n/p^e\rfloor - \lfloor k/p^e\rfloor -
\lfloor (n-k)/p^e\rfloor\big)$. Throughout, $1 \le i < j \le n/2$.

**Lemma 1 (brief's (a), true).** *If $p$ is prime and $n-j < p \le n$,
then $v_p\binom{n}{j} = 1$; in particular $p \mid \binom{n}{j}$.*
Proof. $p > n-j \ge n/2$, so $p > n/2$: $\lfloor n/p \rfloor = 1$ and
all $e \ge 2$ terms vanish ($p^2 > n$). $\lfloor j/p \rfloor = 0$
($j \le n/2 < p$) and $\lfloor (n-j)/p \rfloor = 0$ ($n-j < p$).
Hence $v_p = 1 - 0 - 0 = 1$. ∎

**Lemma 2 (brief's (b), true).** *If $p$ is prime and $n-i < p \le n$,
then $v_p\binom{n}{i} = 1$; in particular $p \mid \binom{n}{i}$.*
Proof. Identical, with $i$ in place of $j$: $p > n-i \ge n/2$ gives
$\lfloor n/p\rfloor = 1$, $\lfloor i/p \rfloor = 0$,
$\lfloor (n-i)/p \rfloor = 0$. ∎

**Lemma 3 (brief's (c) is false; correct form).** The brief asserts
$(n-j,n] \subseteq (n-i,n]$ and concludes every prime in $(n-j,n]$
divides both coefficients. In fact, since $j > i$,
$(n-i,n] \subsetneq (n-j,n]$: the inclusion is reversed, and the
conclusion fails on the annulus $(n-j,\, n-i]$:

* (i) If $p \in (n-i, n]$, then $p \mid \binom{n}{i}$ and
  $p \mid \binom{n}{j}$ (Lemmas 1–2, using $(n-i,n] \subseteq (n-j,n]$),
  and $p > n/2 \ge j > i$, so $p \ge i$: the triple $(n,i,j)$ is **not**
  a counterexample.
* (ii) If $p \in (n-j, n-i]$, then $p \mid \binom{n}{j}$ but
  $p \nmid \binom{n}{i}$. Proof: $p > n/2$ as before, so
  $v_p\binom{n}{i} = 1 - 0 - \lfloor (n-i)/p \rfloor$, and
  $p \le n-i$ with $n-i < n < 2p$ gives $\lfloor (n-i)/p \rfloor = 1$,
  hence $v_p = 0$. ∎
* (iii) Counterexample to the brief's (c): $n=20$, $i=3$, $j=9$,
  $p=13 \in (n-j, n] = (11,20]$. Then $13 \mid \binom{20}{9} = 167960$
  but $13 \nmid \binom{20}{3} = 1140$ (indeed $13 \in (n-j, n-i] =
  (11,17]$, case (ii)). Verified by direct Legendre computation, and
  the trichotomy (i)/(ii) was verified on 3000 random triples
  (§6 script).

**Corollary (gap forcing, corrected).** *If $(n,i,j)$ is a
counterexample, then $(n-i, n]$ contains no prime. Equivalently,
writing $p_-(n)$ for the largest prime $\le n$ (with the convention
that if $n$ is prime the interval contains $n$ and there is nothing to
prove), $p_-(n) \le n-i$, and the prime gap straddling $n$,
$p_+ - p_-$ where $p_+$ is the next prime after $p_-$, satisfies
$p_+ - p_- \ge i+1$.*
Proof. If a prime lay in $(n-i,n]$, Lemma 3(i) would make it a common
divisor $\ge i$. So $p_-(n) \le n-i$; since $p_+ > n$,
$p_+ - p_- \ge (n+1) - (n-i) = i+1$. ∎

Remark (sharpness of the mechanism, brief's item (iii)). The set
$(n-i,n] \setminus (n-j,n]$ in the brief is **empty** (the inclusion
goes the other way), so there is no extra case there. The real
boundary is Lemma 3(ii): primes in $(n-j, n-i]$ are "wasted" — they
divide $\binom{n}{j}$ only. One step lower, primes $p$ with
$n/2 < p \le n-j$ do not even divide $\binom{n}{j}$:
$v_p\binom{n}{j} = 1 - 0 - 1 = 0$ since $\lfloor (n-j)/p \rfloor = 1$.
Thus among primes $> n/2$, the common divisors of the two coefficients
are *exactly* the primes in $(n-i,n]$; the gap condition in the
Corollary is not an artifact of the proof but the exact reach of the
single-hit mechanism. Common primes $\le n/2$ (where $v_p$ involves
genuine digit/carry analysis — S4 territory) are invisible to this
route: the condition is sufficient, never necessary (see §5, item 4).

**Lemma 4 (uniform bound on $N(i,j)$).** *For all $1 \le i < j$,
$N(i,j) < e^{2} j^{2} / i \le e^{2} j^{2}$.*
Proof. $\binom{2j}{2(j-i)} = \binom{2j}{2i} \le (ej/i)^{2i}$ by
$\binom{N}{K} \le (eN/K)^K$. Also $\binom{n}{i} \ge (n/i)^i$, since
each factor $(n-t)/(i-t) \ge n/i$ for $0 \le t < i \le n$. If
$n \ge e^{2}j^{2}/i$ then $(n/i)^i \ge (ej/i)^{2i} \ge
\binom{2j}{2(j-i)}$, so $\binom{n}{i} \ge \binom{2j}{2(j-i)}$ and
$n > N(i,j)$. (The bound is within a factor $e$ of the truth:
Stirling gives $N(i,j) \approx e j^{2}/i$ when $i \ll j$; exactly,
$N(1,j) = j(2j-1) - 1$.) ∎

**Definition.** $G(X) := \max\{p_{k+1} - p_k : p_k < X\}$, the maximal
prime gap with lower end below $X$.

**Theorem A (table criterion).** *Let $(i,j)$ be any pair with
$j > 3i/2$ (indeed any pair) such that $i \ge G(N(i,j))$. Then there
is no counterexample $(n,i,j)$ for any $n$: for every
$2j \le n \le N(i,j)$ the interval $(n-i,n]$ contains a prime, which
by Lemma 3(i) divides both coefficients; and $n > N(i,j)$ is excluded
by the Phase 0 reduction (outside EEES78 exceptions, which are
discharged directly in RESULTS.md). Hence $(i,j)$ is fully cleared.*
Proof. Suppose $(n-i,n]$ were prime-free for some $n \le N(i,j)$.
Then $n - i \ge n/2 \ge 2$, so $p_-(n)$ exists, $p_-(n) \le n-i <
N(i,j)$, and by the Corollary the gap $p_+ - p_- \ge i+1$ is counted
in $G(N(i,j))$, giving $G(N(i,j)) \ge i+1 > i$, contradiction. ∎

## 3. Prime-gap inputs (verified citations)

All $\log$ are natural. "Prime in $(x, x(1+\delta(x))]$" statements are
converted to our backward form as follows: applying the statement at
$x = n-i$ yields a prime in $(n-i,\ (n-i)(1+\delta(n-i))]$; it lies in
$(n-i, n]$ as soon as $i \ge (n-i)\,\delta(n-i)$. Since
$x \mapsto x/\log^m x$ is increasing for $x > e^m$, the clean
sufficient condition $i \ge n\,\delta(n)$ (same $m$) also works.

*Explicit theorems* (table verified against the survey in Ford–et al.
style compilations and Dusart's papers; constants cross-checked in
two independent secondary sources):

| Source | Threshold $x_0$ | Prime in $(x,\ x(1 + C/\log^m x)]$ |
|---|---|---|
| Dusart 2010, arXiv:1002.0442 | $x \ge 396738$ | $C = 1/25,\ m = 2$ |
| Trudgian 2014, arXiv:1401.2689 | $x \ge 2898329$ | $C = 1/111,\ m = 2$ |
| Dusart 2018, *Ramanujan J.* 45, Cor. 5.5 | $x \ge 468991632$ | $C = 1/5000,\ m = 2$ |
| Dusart 2018, *Ramanujan J.* 45, Prop. 5.4 | $x \ge 89693$ | $C = 1,\ m = 3$ |
| Axler 2017, arXiv:1703.08032, Thm 4.1 | $x \ge 6034256$ | $C = 0.087,\ m = 3$ |

The $m=3$ bounds dominate the $m=2$ bounds whenever
$\log x < 435$ (Axler vs. Dusart 2018), i.e. in every range that will
ever be computed; the $1/25$ form is retained as the headline because
the brief asked for it and because its threshold is the smallest of
the $m=2$ family. Bertrand's postulate is the degenerate case
$\delta = 1$ and recovers nothing (it gives a prime in $(n-i,n]$ only
if $i \ge n-i$, i.e. $i \ge n/2$, excluded).

*Exhaustive tables.* Oliveira e Silva, Herzog & Pardi, *Math. Comp.*
83 (2014): all prime gaps below $4\cdot 10^{18}$ computed; the maximal
gap is $1476$, first occurring after the prime $1425172824437699411$.
Hence $G(X) \le 1476$ for all $X \le 4\cdot 10^{18}$. Record
(first-occurrence maximal) gaps used below, from the same tables
(OEIS A002386/A005250): gap $36$ after $9551$ (so $G(X) = 36$ for
$9551 < X \le 15683$); $72$ after $31397$; $86$ after $155921$; $96$
after $360653$; $112$ after $370261$; $114$ after $492113$; $118$
after $1349533$; $132$ after $1357201$; $148$ after $2010733$; $154$
after $4652353$; $180$ after $17051707$; $222$ after $122164747$.

*Asymptotic (non-explicit) input, for the breaking-point discussion
only.* Baker–Harman–Pintz 2001: $p_{k+1} - p_k \ll p_k^{0.525}$ for
sufficiently large $p_k$ — no explicit threshold, so it yields no
explicit closure, only the asymptotic region $i \gtrsim N(i,j)^{0.525}$.

## 4. Partial closures derived

**Theorem B (explicit theorem region; the brief's region with $j$
corrected to $i$).** *There is no counterexample $(n,i,j)$ in any of
the following regions (all $i<j\le n/2$):*

* (B1) $n - i \ge 396738$ and $i \ge \dfrac{n-i}{25\log^2(n-i)}$
  (Dusart 2010). In particular the clean region
  $\{\, i > n / (25 (\log n)^2),\ n - i \ge 396738 \,\}$ is
  counterexample-free.
* (B2) $n - i \ge 468991632$ and $i \ge \dfrac{n-i}{5000\log^2(n-i)}$
  (Dusart 2018).
* (B3) $n - i \ge 6034256$ and $i \ge \dfrac{0.087\,(n-i)}{\log^3(n-i)}$
  (Axler 2017) — the strongest of the three for $\log n < 435$.

*Proof.* Corollary + the conversion above. ∎

This region is about $i$ vs. $n$ and is genuinely complementary to
$j \le 3i/2$ ($j$ vs. $i$): e.g. $i = n/3$, $j = 0.49n$ has
$j > 3i/2$ (open region) but lies in (B3) for all large $n$. Note,
however, that for $n \le 4\cdot 10^{18}$ Theorem A with the tables is
vastly stronger than Theorem B (it needs only $i \ge 1476$); Theorem B
earns its keep only for $n > 4\cdot 10^{18}$, see §5.

**Theorem C (table closures; new fully cleared families).**

* (C1) *Every open pair $(i,j)$ with $i \ge 1476$ and
  $j \le 7.35\cdot 10^{8}$ is fully cleared.* Proof: Lemma 4 gives
  $N(i,j) < e^2 j^2 \le 3.99\cdot 10^{18} < 4\cdot 10^{18}$, so
  $G(N(i,j)) \le 1476 \le i$; apply Theorem A. ∎
* (C2) *Every open pair with $132 \le i \le 1000$ and $j \le 5000$ is
  fully cleared.* Proof: Lemma 4 gives
  $N(i,j) < e^2 j^2/i \le e^2 \cdot 5000^2/132 < 1.40\cdot 10^{6}$,
  and the record table gives $G(1.40\cdot10^6) = 132$ (gap $132$ after
  $1357201$; next record $148$ after $2010733$), so
  $G(N(i,j)) \le 132 \le i$; apply Theorem A. ∎ In particular **all
  2,891,106 partially covered pairs with $132 \le i \le 1000$
  (those with $J_i < j \le 5000$, $J_i$ the sweep's maximal cleared
  $j$) are now fully cleared** — proved uniformly, with no per-pair
  computation. The same argument with $G \le 222$ clears every open
  pair with $222 \le i$ and $e^2j^2/i \le 1.90\cdot10^{8}$ (e.g. all
  $j \le 5000$, $i \ge 222$ is subsumed by (C2)'s sharper bound).
* (C3) *Per-pair criterion (decidable):* any specific pair with
  $N(i,j) \le 4\cdot 10^{18}$ and $i \ge G(N(i,j))$ is fully cleared,
  where $N(i,j)$ is computed exactly by Phase 0's binary search and
  $G$ is read off the record table. E.g. the exact frontier
  computation of §6: every $(i, 71)$ with $18 \le i \le 47$ satisfies
  the criterion (the $i \le 17$ cases have $N(i,71) \le 10010$ and,
  except $(1,71)$, already satisfy $N \le 10000$, i.e. were cleared by
  the sweep anyway).

Interaction with the reduction, summarised: a counterexample needs
simultaneously $n \le N(i,j)$ and a gap $\ge i+1$ at $n$. The tables
say gaps $\ge 1477$ do not occur at all below $4\cdot10^{18}$, and
gaps $\ge g$ first occur at explicitly tabulated primes; Theorem A is
exactly the comparison of these two bounds. What the route adds over
the sweep is therefore concentrated where $N(i,j)$ is large but $i$
is not small: the sweep's $3{,}480{,}759$ partially covered pairs are
precisely those with $N(i,j) > 10000$, and (C2) discharges all of them
with $i \ge 132$ in one stroke.

## 5. Honest breaking point

1. **Small $i$: the route is nearly blind.** The criterion is
   $i \ge G(N(i,j))$, and $G$ grows (heuristically $\sim (\log N)^2$,
   tabulated up to $1476$). For fixed $i$ and $j \to \infty$,
   $N(i,j) \approx e j^2/i \to \infty$ while $i$ stays put, so every
   fixed-$i$ family eventually exits the criterion's reach as far as
   *uniform* arguments go — and the tables rescue it only while
   $N(i,j) \le 4\cdot10^{18}$. Extremal case $i = 1$: $(n-1, n] = \{n\}$
   contains a prime iff $n$ is prime, so the route rules out only
   prime $n$ (where $p = n$ indeed divides both coefficients). For
   composite $n$ it says nothing, for every $j$. §6 measures this:
   only $7.4\%$ of $n \in (10000, N(1,1000)]$ are ruled out.
   Similarly $i = 2$ rules out only $\approx 20\%$ at the frontier.
   Closing $i = 1$ for all $j$ via gaps alone would require a prime
   in $(n-1,n]$ for composite $n$ — impossible — so $i=1$ must be
   closed by another route entirely (for the record: it is elementary
   by other means, since a prime divisor of $n$ can be chased into
   $\binom{n}{j}$ except in controllable cases; that is not this
   paper's route).
2. **Beyond the tables ($N(i,j) > 4\cdot 10^{18}$).** Such pairs
   exist: by Lemma 4's sharp form $N(i,j) \approx ej^2/i$, so
   $N(i,j) > 4\cdot10^{18}$ once $j \gtrsim \sqrt{1.47\cdot10^{18}\, i}$
   (e.g. $i = 1476$, $j \gtrsim 4.7\cdot10^{10}$; the crude proved bound
   certifies $N \le 4\cdot10^{18}$ only up to $j = 7.35\cdot10^{8}$).
   For $n \in (4\cdot10^{18}, N(i,j)]$ no gap table exists, and
   Theorem B covers only the initial segment
   $n - i \lesssim 5000\, i \log^2(n-i)$ (resp. $i\log^3$ scale): for
   $i = 1476$ that segment ends near $n \approx 3\cdot10^{9}$, far
   *inside* the tabulated range, so the theorems add nothing beyond
   the tables until $5000\, i \log^2(\cdot) > 4\cdot10^{18}$, i.e.
   roughly $i \gtrsim 10^{12}$. The uncovered regime is therefore:
   pairs with $i \lesssim 10^{12}$ and $N(i,j) > 4\cdot10^{18}$, at
   $n$ above both the tables and the Dusart segment. Nothing known
   excludes a gap $\ge i+1$ there — such gaps provably *exist*
   somewhere (factorial construction), just at uncontrolled locations.
3. **Even the best theory stops at $n^{0.525}$.** Baker–Harman–Pintz
   would (if made explicit) clear pairs with
   $i \gtrsim N(i,j)^{0.525}$. Pairs with $i \ll N(i,j)^{0.525}$ —
   including every fixed-$i$ family, since $N \approx ej^2/i$ gives
   $N^{0.525} \approx j^{1.05} \gg i$ — are untouched by any known
   gap theorem. A Cramér-strength bound $G(X) = O((\log X)^2)$ would
   clear all $i \gtrsim (\log N(i,j))^2$ and, with the tables, would
   be devastating; it is conjectural, and this paper does not use it.
4. **Sufficiency, not necessity — in-range failures exist.** The
   route detects only common primes $> n/2$. Concrete failure inside
   an already-swept interval: $i = 17$, $j = 71$, $n = 540 \le
   N(17,71) = 621$: $(540-17, 540] = (523, 540]$ is prime-free
   ($523$ and $541$ are consecutive primes), so the route is silent,
   yet the triple is fine (sweep-verified; the common prime is
   $\le n/2$). Any $n$ sitting in a gap $\ge i+1$ below $N(i,j)$ is
   such a blind spot, and for $i \le 36$ these occur already below
   $n = 15683$. This is the precise sense in which S3 must be paired
   with a small-prime route (S4-style digit analysis) to go further.

post-sweep triples, concentrated entirely in the larger-$i$ pairs
where it is total.

**Bottom line.** Corrected, the gap route is a real but $i$-sided
weapon: Theorems B and C fully clear explicit infinite families far
beyond the sweep (all $132 \le i \le 1000$, $j \le 5000$; all
$i \ge 1476$, $j \le 7.35\cdot10^8$), it is complementary to both the
$j \le 3i/2$ proof and the $n \le 10000$ sweep, and it provably cannot
touch small fixed $i$ or the post-table regime $N(i,j) > 4\cdot10^{18}$
with $i \lesssim 10^{12}$ — which is where the campaign's other routes
must go.
