# Strategy S5 — Mine the identity harder: surplus, two-sided, and iterated bookkeeping

**Route:** take the single identity
$C(n,i)\,C(n-i,\,j-i) = C(n,j)\,C(j,i)$ and extract *everything* it gives
$p$-adically — the full surplus of each side over the gcd, the symmetric
statement for the $j$-side, the ratio $R = C(n,j)/C(n,i)$ prime by prime,
the exact small-prime part $u$ (not just the EEES78 bound on it), and
iterated/chain applications — then determine, honestly, which of these
actually close anything that Phase 0's stated reduction does not.

**Verdict, up front:** mixed, and the split is clean.
(i) The **two-sided** extension *collapses*: the $j$-side divisibility
gives, as a size consequence, exactly the same inequality
$\sqrt{C(n,i)} < C(j,\,j-i)$ as the $i$-side (Proposition 3).
(ii) **Iteration collapses for single pairs**: chain applications only
constrain *simultaneous* failures at several pairs, and the three-term
identity is strictly dominated by the direct pair lemma (§7).
(iii) But the strengthened bookkeeping **does** add real closures, from
two other directions: the *surplus lemma* (Lemma 1, a genuine
strengthening of the key lemma) combined with discarding Phase 0's
lossy Vandermonde step gives the **square reduction**
$C(n,i) < C(j,\,j-i)^2$, which closes an explicit infinite boundary
family $j = \lfloor 3i/2\rfloor + 1$ that Phase 0's reduction
$C(n,i) < C(2j,\,2(j-i))$ does not close (§4); and *exact* bookkeeping
of the small part $u$ (prime-power bound, Lemma 6) closes the families
$i = 1$ and $i = 2$ **for all $j$**, all $i$-rough triples, and improves
the fixed-$i$ reduction exponent from $2$ to $i/(i-s)$ (§5).
All new divisibility claims are verified on all $1{,}113{,}775$ triples
with $n \le 300$ (§9).

## 0. Bookkeeping (used throughout)

Fix $1 \le i < j \le n/2$, write $d = j-i$,
$A = C(n,i)$, $B = C(n,j)$, and for a prime $p$

$$\alpha_p = v_p(A),\qquad \beta_p = v_p(B),\qquad
\gamma_p = v_p(C(n-i,d)),\qquad \delta_p = v_p(C(j,i)).$$

Note $C(j,i) = C(j,d)$. The basic identity and its $p$-adic form:

$$A\cdot C(n-i,d) = B\cdot C(j,i),
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \alpha_p + \gamma_p = \beta_p + \delta_p
\ \ \text{for all } p. \tag{ID}$$

Decompose $A = u\,v$ with $v = V_i(n) = \prod_{p\ge i} p^{\alpha_p}$ and
$u = \prod_{p<i} p^{\alpha_p}$ (so $\gcd(u,v)=1$).
Set

$$g_0 = \gcd(A,B),\qquad
g' = A/g_0 \ \ (\text{the } i\text{-side surplus}),\qquad
e' = B/g_0 \ \ (\text{the } j\text{-side surplus}),$$

so $v_p(g') = \max(0, \alpha_p-\beta_p)$ and
$v_p(e') = \max(0, \beta_p-\alpha_p)$.
A **counterexample** is a triple with $\gcd(v, B) = 1$, i.e.
$\beta_p = 0$ for every $p \ge i$ with $\alpha_p \ge 1$.
EEES78 input, where used: if $(n,i)$ is not one of the 12 exception
pairs, $u < v$, i.e. $v^2 > A$. Every use of it below is flagged;
several results need no EEES at all.

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

Phase 0 applied the key lemma to the single divisor $v$ and then
*weakened* the resulting bound $v \le C(j,d)$ by squaring and inserting
the Vandermonde sum, ending at $C(n,i) < C(2j,2d)$. The surplus
$g' \ge v$ (under a counterexample) is the largest divisor of $A$ that
the identity can force into $C(j,i)$; the symmetric surplus $e'$ is
forced into $C(n-i,d)$; the ratio $R = B/A$ tells exactly, prime by
prime, *why* a prime of $v$ can fail to divide $B$; and the exact size
of $u$ — not merely $u < v$ — is sometimes $1$ or at most a power of
$n$. Working all four ledgers at once shows precisely where the
identity's strength ends: the two-sided and iterated ledgers balance
to the same single inequality, while the surplus and exact-$u$ ledgers
pay out in the boundary layer $j \approx 3i/2$ and at small $i$,
respectively.

## 2. Sub-route 1: the surplus $g'$

**Lemma 1 (surplus divisibility, unconditional).**
$g' \mid C(j,i)$ and $e' \mid C(n-i,d)$, for *every* triple
(no counterexample assumption, no coprimality assumption).

*Proof.* For $g'$: if $\alpha_p > \beta_p$, (ID) gives
$\delta_p = \alpha_p - \beta_p + \gamma_p \ge \alpha_p - \beta_p
= v_p(g')$ since $\gamma_p \ge 0$; if $\alpha_p \le \beta_p$ then
$v_p(g') = 0 \le \delta_p$. Hence $v_p(g') \le \delta_p$ for all $p$.
For $e'$: if $\beta_p > \alpha_p$, (ID) gives
$\gamma_p = \beta_p - \alpha_p + \delta_p \ge \beta_p - \alpha_p
= v_p(e')$; else $v_p(e') = 0$. $\square$

**This is strictly stronger than the key lemma.** The key lemma
requires $\gcd(d, B) = 1$; $g'$ need not be coprime to $B$.
Example (verified, §9 (V9)): $(n,i,j) = (10,2,4)$: $A = 45$, $B = 210$,
$g_0 = 15$, $g' = 3$, and $\gcd(g', B) = 3 > 1$ — the key lemma does
not apply to $d = g'$ — yet $g' = 3 \mid C(4,2) = 6$. (Also
$e' = 14 \mid C(8,2) = 28$.) Phase 0's test (B) verified the coprime
part of $v$; Lemma 1 is the full surplus statement.

**Specialisation under a counterexample.** If $\gcd(v,B)=1$ then:
(a) $v_p(g_0) = \min(\alpha_p,\beta_p) = 0$ for all $p \ge i$, so
$g_0$ is supported on primes $< i$ and $g_0 \mid u$;
(b) $v_p(g') = \alpha_p$ for $p \ge i$, so $v \mid g'$, and writing

$$g' = v\,t,\qquad
t := \prod_{p<i} p^{\max(0,\,\alpha_p-\beta_p)},$$

we have $t \mid u$ and in fact $u = g_0\,t$ (prime by prime for
$p < i$: $\alpha_p = \min(\alpha_p,\beta_p) + \max(0,\alpha_p-\beta_p)$).

**Precise failure point — no better lower bound on $g'$ is available
from this system.** EEES78 gives $g' \ge A/g_0 \ge A/u = v$ (using
$g_0 \mid u$). The gain factor over $v$ is exactly
$t = u/g_0 \in [1, u]$, and $t = 1$ iff $\beta_p \ge \alpha_p$ for all
$p < i$ (the small part of $A$ is fully shared with $B$). Nothing in
(ID), Lemma 1, or EEES78 excludes $t = 1$: the pattern is realised at
the level of the small primes already in ordinary triples, e.g.
$(n,i,j) = (12,3,5)$ has $\alpha_2 = 2 \le 3 = \beta_2$ for the sole
prime $2 < 3$ (that triple is saved by a large prime, a separate
ledger). Hence: *any* improvement of the lower bound $g' \ge v$ is
exactly equivalent to a new theorem of the form $g_0 < u$ — an upper
bound on the shared small part — which the identity does not supply.
This is where sub-route 1 stops, and it stops for a stated reason,
not for lack of trying.

## 3. Sub-route 1, other side: $e'$ and the two-sided collapse

**Proposition 3 (two-sided size collapse).** Assume a counterexample
and EEES78 non-exception. Then the size consequence of
$e' \mid C(n-i,d)$ is *exactly* the inequality
$\sqrt{A} < C(j,d)$ — the same consequence as Phase 0's
$v \le C(j,d)$. The two-sided system therefore yields no stronger
size threshold than the one-sided one.

*Proof.* By §2(a), $g_0 \mid u$, so $g_0 \le u < \sqrt{A}$ (EEES78).
Divisibility gives $e' \le C(n-i,d)$, i.e.
$B = g_0 e' \le g_0\,C(n-i,d) < \sqrt{A}\;C(n-i,d)$.
By (ID), $C(n-i,d) = B\,C(j,d)/A$; substituting and cancelling
$B > 0$ gives $1 < C(j,d)/\sqrt{A}$. Conversely the same identity
shows $e' \le C(n-i,d) \iff g' \le C(j,d)$ as size statements:
$B \le g_0 C(n-i,d)$ rearranges, via $C(n-i,d) = B\,C(j,d)/A$ and
$A/g_0 = g'$, to $g' \le C(j,d)$. $\square$

So the symmetric statement the task asked to derive is true and clean
— if $e \mid B$ with $\gcd(e,A)=1$ then $e \mid C(n-i,d)$ (a special
case of Lemma 1 for $e'$) — but as *bookkeeping* it balances: the
$j$-side ledger and the $i$-side ledger are two presentations of one
inequality. Any genuinely two-sided gain would have to come from
information about $g_0$ beyond $g_0 \mid u$, the same missing input
as in §2.

## 4. First real gain: the square reduction and the boundary family

**Proposition 4 (square reduction).** Assume a counterexample and
EEES78 non-exception. Then

$$A \;<\; v^2 \;\le\; g'^2 \;\le\; C(j,d)^2,
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad C(n,i) < C(j,\,j-i)^2. \tag{SQ}$$

*Proof.* $v \mid g'$ (§2(b)) and $g' \mid C(j,d)$ (Lemma 1) give
$v \le C(j,d)$; EEES78 gives $v^2 > A$. $\square$

Phase 0's stated reduction instead concludes
$C(n,i) < C(2j,2d)$, inserting the Vandermonde strict inequality
$C(j,d)^2 < C(2j,2d)$ — a *weakening* of (SQ). (Honesty note: the
inequality $v^2 \le C(j,d)^2$ itself appears as Steps 3–4 of Phase 0;
what is new here is (i) using (SQ) itself as the reduction, (ii) the
explicit family below, and (iii) everything in §5, which is outside
the EEES route entirely.) Define
$N_{\mathrm{sq}}(i,j) = \max\{n : C(n,i) < C(j,d)^2\}$; a
counterexample satisfies $n \le \min(N(i,j), N_{\mathrm{sq}}(i,j))$,
and a pair is closed by (SQ) alone iff $C(2j,i) \ge C(j,d)^2$
(monotonicity of $C(\cdot,i)$), a condition Phase 0's reduction
cannot certify for any $j > 3i/2$.

**Worked examples.** $(i,j) = (3,5)$: Phase 0 gives
$N(3,5) = 11$ (target $C(10,4) = 210$; interval $[10,11]$ nonempty),
while $C(10,3) = 120 \ge C(5,2)^2 = 100$: closed by (SQ) for all
$n \ge 10$, no sweep needed. $(i,j)=(5,8)$: $N(5,8) = 17$
(target $C(16,6) = 8008$), while $C(16,5) = 4368 \ge C(8,3)^2 = 3136$:
closed by (SQ). (§9 (V8).)

**Theorem 5 (infinite boundary family).** Let
$j = \lfloor 3i/2 \rfloor + 1$. For every odd $i \ge 3$ and every
even $i \ge 10$, $C(2j,i) \ge C(j,\,j-i)^2$, while
$C(2j,i) < C(2j,\,2(j-i))$. Hence (SQ) closes all these pairs
(for all $n$, EEES78 proviso below), and Phase 0's stated reduction
closes none of them.

*Proof.* Phase 0 non-closure: odd case $i = 2m+1$, $j = 3m+2$:
$C(2j,\,2d)/C(2j,i) = (4m+3)/(2m+2) > 1$ since $2d = 2m+2$,
$i = 2m+1$, $2j = 6m+4$. Even case $i = 2m$, $j = 3m+1$:
$C(2j,\,2d)/C(2j,i) = \frac{(4m+2)(4m+1)}{(2m+1)(2m+2)} > 1$.
Square closure, odd case: set
$R_m = C(6m+4,\,2m+1)\,/\,C(3m+2,\,m+1)^2$ (note
$C(j,d) = C(3m+2, m+1)$). Then $R_1 = 120/100 = 6/5 > 1$ and, by
expanding the binomial ratios,

$$\frac{R_{m+1}}{R_m} =
\frac{2(m+2)^2(2m+3)(6m+5)(6m+7)}
     {(m+1)(3m+4)(3m+5)(4m+5)(4m+7)},\qquad
\frac{R_{m+1}}{R_m} - 1 =
\frac{72m^4 + 369m^3 + 678m^2 + 523m + 140}
     {(m+1)(3m+4)(3m+5)(4m+5)(4m+7)} > 0$$

for all $m \ge 1$ (numerator coefficients all positive). So
$R_m > 1$ for all $m \ge 1$. Even case:
$R_m = C(6m+2,\,2m)\,/\,C(3m+1,\,m+1)^2$ satisfies

$$\frac{R_{m+1}}{R_m} =
\frac{2(m+2)^2(2m+1)^2(6m+5)(6m+7)}
     {(m+1)(2m+3)(3m+2)(3m+4)(4m+3)(4m+5)},\qquad
\frac{R_{m+1}}{R_m} - 1 =
\frac{144m^5 + 546m^4 + 617m^3 + 91m^2 - 202m - 80}
     {(m+1)(2m+3)(3m+2)(3m+4)(4m+3)(4m+5)},$$

whose numerator is positive for $m \ge 1$ (indeed
$546m^4 \ge 546m \ge 202m + 80$ for $m \ge 1$, and the remaining
terms are positive). Hence $R_m$ is strictly increasing, and
$R_5 = 64512240/64128064 > 1$ (the pair $(10,16)$), so $R_m > 1$
for all $m \ge 5$. $\square$

*EEES78 proviso:* the argument needs $v^2 > A$ at the given $(n,i)$.
The only EEES78 exception pair falling inside this family with
$n \ge 2j$ is $(n,i,j) = (56,13,20)$; it is discharged directly by
the Stage 2 sweep (RESULTS.md). All other family pairs are
unconditional.

*Remark (why only a boundary layer).* At any *fixed* ratio
$c = j/i > 3/2$, Stirling gives
$\log C(2j,i) - 2\log C(j,d) \sim
2ci\,[H(1/(2c)) - H(1 - 1/c)] < 0$ (the bracket is negative because
$H$ is increasing on $[0,\tfrac12]$ and symmetric, and
$1/(2c) < \min(1-1/c,\, 1/c)$ in the relevant ranges), so (SQ) fails
for large $i$ at fixed $c$. The gain in Theorem 5 is a prefactor
($\asymp i^{1/2}$) effect living in the layer $j - 3i/2 = O(1)$,
exactly where Phase 0's Vandermonde loss ($\asymp$ the same
prefactor) was spent. This does not contradict fixed-ratio entropy
barriers for size-only arguments; it recovers precisely the factor
Phase 0 threw away.

## 5. Second real gain: exact $u$ bookkeeping (no EEES78)

**Lemma 6 (prime-power bound).** For every prime $p$ and
$1 \le k \le n$, $v_p(C(n,k)) \le \lfloor \log_p n \rfloor$; hence
$p^{v_p(C(n,k))} \le n$.

*Proof.* Legendre:
$v_p(C(n,k)) = \sum_{e\ge1}
\big(\lfloor n/p^e\rfloor - \lfloor k/p^e\rfloor
- \lfloor (n-k)/p^e\rfloor\big)$.
Each bracket is $0$ or $1$ (it is
$\lfloor x+y\rfloor - \lfloor x\rfloor - \lfloor y\rfloor$ for
$x = k/p^e$, $y = (n-k)/p^e$), and brackets with $p^e > n$ vanish,
leaving at most $\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor$ nonzero terms. $\square$

**Corollary (exact reduction).** Let $s = \#\{p \text{ prime} : p < i\}$
(so $s \le \lfloor i/2\rfloor$: the odd primes $< i$ are distinct odd
numbers in $[3, i-1]$). Then $u \le n^{s}$, and any counterexample
satisfies, with **no EEES78 hypothesis**,

$$C(n,i) = u\,v \;\le\; n^{s}\, C(j,d). \tag{U}$$

Define $N_u(i,j) = \max\{n \ge 2j : C(n,i) \le n^s C(j,d)\}$ (finite:
$C(n,i)/n^s = \frac{n^{i-s}}{i!}\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}(1 - k/n) \to \infty$
since $s < i$, and the displayed product form shows the ratio is
strictly increasing in $n$, so (U) also gives a pair-closure test at
$n = 2j$). A counterexample satisfies
$n \le \min(N(i,j),\, N_{\mathrm{sq}}(i,j),\, N_u(i,j))$.

*Fixed-$i$ growth.* For fixed $i$ and $j \to \infty$, (U) plus
$C(j,i) \le j^i$ and $C(n,i) \ge (n/(2i))^i$ for $n \ge 2i$ gives
$n \le 2i\cdot j^{\,i/(i-s)}$, versus Phase 0's $O_i(j^2)$. The
exponent $i/(i-s)$ is $< 2$ iff $s < i/2$: e.g. $i=3$: $3/2$;
$i=5$: $5/3$; $i=7$: $7/4$; $i=10$: $5/3$; it equals $2$ at
$i \in \{4,6,8\}$ ($s = i/2$) and tends to $1$ as $i \to \infty$.
So (U) is asymptotically the better reduction for most fixed $i$,
though it closes no new pair by itself except through $s = 0$:

**Theorem 7 ($u = 1$ closures).** If $u = 1$ — i.e. $C(n,i)$ has no
prime factor $< i$ — then $(n,i,j)$ is not a counterexample for any
$j$. In particular **$i = 1$ and $i = 2$ are settled for all $j$ and
all $n$** (there are no primes $< i$, so $u = 1$ identically).

*Proof.* A counterexample would give, by the key lemma (or Lemma 1),
$v \mid C(j,d)$ with $v = A = C(n,i)$. But $n > j$ implies
$C(n,i) > C(j,i) = C(j,d) > 0$, and a positive integer cannot divide
a smaller positive integer. No EEES78 is used. $\square$

Note Phase 0's reduction + EEES78 does not close a single pair with
$i \in \{1,2\}$, $j > 3i/2$ (its interval is nonempty, e.g.
$N(1,j) \approx 2j^2$ per PHASE0.md); Theorem 7 closes the two
infinite families $\{(1,j)\}_j$ and $\{(2,j)\}_j$ outright.
A further $u=1$ subfamily: for $i = 3$, $u$ is the $2$-part of
$C(n,3)$, and $v_2(n(n-1)(n-2)) = 1$ iff $n \equiv 3 \pmod 4$
(direct check of the four residue classes: the sole even factor
$n-1 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$ contributes one factor of $2$, and in the
other three classes some factor is divisible by $4$), so
$v_2(C(n,3)) = v_2(n(n-1)(n-2)) - v_2(6) = 0$, i.e. $C(n,3)$ is
odd, exactly when $n \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. Every triple with $i = 3$
and $n \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ is therefore closed by Theorem 7 as well.

## 6. Sub-route 3: the ratio $R$ prime by prime

Write $B = A\cdot R$ with
$R = \mathrm{Num}/\mathrm{Den}$,
$\mathrm{Num} = (n-i)!/(n-j)! = \prod_{t=1}^{d}(n-i-t+1)$,
$\mathrm{Den} = j!/i! = \prod_{t=1}^{d}(i+t)$ (a rational identity;
$R$ need not be an integer). Then for every prime $p$,

$$\beta_p = \alpha_p + v_p(\mathrm{Num}) - v_p(\mathrm{Den}).
\tag{R}$$

**Exact failure criterion.** For $p \ge i$ with $\alpha_p \ge 1$:
$p \nmid B$ iff $\beta_p = 0$ iff
$v_p(\mathrm{Den}) = \alpha_p + v_p(\mathrm{Num}) \ge \alpha_p$.
In particular a counterexample forces $v \mid \mathrm{Den}$
(componentwise on the support of $v$).

**Theorem 8 (persistence).** If $\alpha_p \ge 1$ for some prime
$p \ge i$ with $v_p(\mathrm{Den}) = 0$, then $p \mid B$ — the prime
persists. This happens in exactly the following cases:
(a) $p > j$ (every factor of $\mathrm{Den}$ is $\le j < p$, and higher
powers are multiples of $p$); (b) $p = i$ and $j < 2i$ (the multiples
of $i$ in $(i, j]$ would have to be $\ge 2i > j$).
Consequently a counterexample has $\operatorname{supp}(v) \subseteq
[i, j]$: every prime factor of $v$ lies in $[i,j]$.

*Proof.* (R) with $v_p(\mathrm{Den}) = 0$ gives
$\beta_p = \alpha_p + v_p(\mathrm{Num}) \ge \alpha_p \ge 1$.
For (a): $v_p(\mathrm{Den}) = v_p(j!) - v_p(i!) = 0$ since no
multiple of $p$ lies in $(i, j] \subseteq [1,j]$. For (b): same
argument with the interval $(i,j]$ and least multiple $2i$. The
support conclusion is (a) contraposed over all $p \mid v$. $\square$

Note the support conclusion is also implied by $v \mid C(j,d)$
(prime divisors of $C(j,d)$ are $\le j$); the $R$-route proves it
independently and, more importantly, gives the *quantitative*
criterion:

**D-test vs C-test (domination).** Call the *D-test* the sufficient
condition "$\alpha_p > v_p(\mathrm{Den})$ for some $p \ge i$"
(it forces $\beta_p \ge 1$ by (R), since $v_p(\mathrm{Num}) \ge 0$),
and the *C-test* the condition "$\alpha_p > \delta_p =
v_p(C(j,i))$ for some $p \ge i$" (it forces
$\beta_p = \alpha_p - \delta_p + \gamma_p \ge 1$ by (ID)).
Since $C(j,i) = \mathrm{Den}/(j-i)!$, we have
$\delta_p = v_p(\mathrm{Den}) - v_p((j-i)!) \le v_p(\mathrm{Den})$:
**the C-test is always at least as strong as the D-test, and
strictly stronger in general.** Example: $(n,i,j) = (16,3,8)$:
$A = 560$, $v = 35$, $\mathrm{Den} = 8!/3! = 6720$,
$C(8,3) = 56$. Here $v \mid \mathrm{Den}$ ($6720 = 35\cdot192$) but
$v \nmid C(8,3)$, and the C-test succeeds (for $p = 5$:
$\alpha_5 = 1 > 0 = \delta_5$, and indeed $5 \mid C(16,8)$) while
the D-test fails for every prime ($v_5(\mathrm{Den}) =
v_7(\mathrm{Den}) = 1 = \alpha$). So the $R$-ledger, mined alone,
is *dominated* by the key-lemma ledger: its failure criterion
$v \mid \mathrm{Den}$ is strictly weaker than $v \mid C(j,d)$
($\mathrm{Den} = C(j,d)\cdot(j-i)!$). Its independent value is
Theorem 8's clean persistence cases and the exact equation
$v_p(\mathrm{Den}) = \alpha_p + v_p(\mathrm{Num})$ localising any
hypothetical failure.

**Exponent caps.** By Lemma 6, every prime power in $v$ satisfies
$p^{\alpha_p} \le n$, and $\alpha_p \le v_p(\mathrm{Den})$ is forced
for failing primes; for $p \in (j/2, j]$ with $p > i$,
$v_p(\mathrm{Den}) = 1$ (only the multiple $p$ itself lies in
$(i,j]$), so a failing such prime must have $\alpha_p = 1$ and
$v_p(\mathrm{Num}) = 0$. These are sharp local constraints but, as
the domination above shows, all are implied by
$\alpha_p \le \delta_p$; they do not yield a new pair closure.

## 7. Sub-route 2: iteration and chains — where it stops

**Lemma 9 (simultaneous-failure chain).** If $(n,i,j)$ and
$(n,i,k)$ are *both* counterexamples ($i<j<k\le n/2$), then
$v \mid \gcd(C(j,i),\, C(k,i))$.

*Proof.* Key lemma applied to each pair. $\square$

This can be much stronger than either bound alone: for
$(i,j,k) = (4,7,9)$, $\gcd(C(7,4), C(9,4)) = \gcd(35,126) = 7$,
so a simultaneous failure would force $v \le 7$, while
$n \ge 2k = 18$ and EEES78 give $v > \sqrt{C(18,4)} > 55$ —
impossible; whereas the pair $(4,7)$ alone is *not* closed by (SQ)
($C(14,4) = 1001 < 35^2$). **But** Lemma 9 constrains only
simultaneous failures. To close the single pair $(i,j)$ one may not
assume failure at $(i,k)$: the truth of the conjecture at $(i,k)$ is
perfectly consistent with failure at $(i,j)$, and if $(i,k)$ is
already *proved* (e.g. fully cleared by the sweep) then the chain
hypothesis is false and yields nothing. Iteration across $k$ is
therefore a statement about configurations, not a pair-closure
tool. (Useful bookkeeping for a minimal-counterexample argument
ordered by $j$, but no such ordering closes a pair for free:
minimality in $j$ gives information about $k < j$, while Lemma 9
needs a failure at some $k$, which minimality excludes below $j$
and cannot supply above $j$.)

**Three-term identity is dominated.** From
$C(n,i)C(n-i,d)C(n-j,k-j) = C(n,k)C(k,j)C(j,i)$, if $d_0 \mid A$ and
$\gcd(d_0, C(n,k)) = 1$ one gets $d_0 \mid C(k,j)C(j,i)$. But
$C(k,j)C(j,i) = C(k,i)\cdot C(k-i,\,j-i)$ (both sides equal
$k!/(i!\,(j-i)!\,(k-j)!)$), so this is strictly weaker than the
direct pair-$(i,k)$ key lemma $d_0 \mid C(k,i)$. Iteration through
the three-term identity loses the factor $C(k-i, j-i)$ and adds
nothing.

**Descent collapses.** Let $a = \min(i, d)$. Since $v \mid C(j,d)$
and every prime of $v$ is $\ge i \ge a$ with exponent at most that
in $C(j,d)$, we have $v \mid V_a(j)$: the counterexample's $v$
embeds in the large part of the *smaller* binomial $C(j,d)$.
Applying EEES78 at $(j,a)$ lower-bounds the container,
$V_a(j) > \sqrt{C(j,a)}$ — the wrong direction: the only upper
bound on $V_a(j)$ is $C(j,d)$ itself, recovering Phase 0's Step 3.
Descent is a restatement, not a recursion.

## 8. Strongest exact partial-closure statement from S5

**Theorem S5.** Fix $1 \le i < j \le n/2$, $d = j - i$,
$s = \#\{p < i\}$, $\alpha_p, \delta_p$ as in §0. If $(n,i,j)$ is a
counterexample, then *all* of the following hold:
(1) $g' \mid C(j,d)$ with $g' = vt$, $t \mid u$, $u = g_0 t$;
(2) $e' \mid C(n-i,d)$ and $g_0 \mid u$;
(3) $\operatorname{supp}(v) \subseteq [i,j]$, and for every
$p \mid v$: $v_p(\mathrm{Den}) = \alpha_p + v_p(\mathrm{Num})$;
(4) $C(n,i) \le n^s C(j,d)$ — unconditionally (no EEES78);
(5) if $(n,i)$ is EEES78-non-exceptional,
$C(n,i) < C(j,d)^2$ and $C(n,i) < C(2j,2d)$.

Consequently $(n,i,j)$ is **not** a counterexample if any of these
sufficient conditions holds: $u = 1$ (in particular $i \le 2$, all
$j$, all $n$); the C-test $\alpha_p > \delta_p$ for some $p \ge i$;
$C(n,i) > n^s C(j,d)$; or, under EEES78-non-exceptionality,
$C(n,i) \ge C(j,d)^2$ — in particular the pair $(i,j)$ is closed
for all $n$ if $C(2j,i) \ge C(j,d)^2$, which holds for the infinite
family $j = \lfloor 3i/2\rfloor + 1$, $i$ odd $\ge 3$ or even
$\ge 10$ (Theorem 5, with the single in-family EEES78 exception
triple $(56,13,20)$ discharged by the Stage 2 sweep), in addition
to Phase 0's region $j \le 3i/2$. And any counterexample obeys the
combined reduction
$n \le \min(N(i,j), N_{\mathrm{sq}}(i,j), N_u(i,j))$.

## 10. Breaking point, summarised

| Component | Adds beyond Phase 0's reduction + EEES78? |
|---|---|
| Surplus $g'$ (Lemma 1) | Stronger *lemma* (no coprimality needed); under a counterexample $g' = vt$ with $t$ uncontrolled in $[1,u]$ — no size gain by itself |
| Two-sided $e'$ | **No** — size-equivalent to the one-sided bound (Prop. 3) |
| Iteration / chains / 3-term | **No** for single pairs — simultaneous configurations only; 3-term dominated; descent reverses ( §7) |
| $R$-valuations | Exact local criterion, but D-test **dominated** by C-test (§6); persistence cases are corollaries |
| Square reduction (SQ) | **Yes** — infinite boundary family $j = \lfloor 3i/2\rfloor+1$ (Thm. 5), pairs $(3,5)$, $(5,8)$, etc. |
| Exact $u$ (Lemma 6, (U)) | **Yes** — $i \in \{1,2\}$ for all $j$ (Thm. 7), $i$-rough triples, fixed-$i$ exponent $i/(i-s) < 2$ for most $i$ |

The identity, mined to the bottom, gives up everything it has at
exactly two places Phase 0 did not spend: the prefactor lost to
Vandermonde near the diagonal, and the exact small-prime part that
EEES78 only bounds by $\sqrt{A}$. The remaining open region
(fixed ratio $j/i > 3/2$, $i \ge 3$, $u \ge 2$) is untouched by any
bookkeeping in this system: closing it needs a genuinely new input —
an upper bound on the shared part $g_0$ relative to $u$, a
strengthened EEES-type lower bound on $v$, or arithmetic input about
$C(n,i)$ beyond its factorisation pattern — and S5's collapse
results (Prop. 3, §7, §6 domination) are the evidence that no
rearrangement of the present identity will substitute for one.
