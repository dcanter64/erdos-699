# S8 — Small-i complete closures and induction on i

**Route:** fixed small $i$, all $j$, all $n$; then induction on $i$.
**Status summary:** $i=1$ **SOLVED** (complete, unconditional). $i=2$ **SOLVED**
(complete, unconditional). $i=3$ **REDUCED** to one explicit Diophantine
system (LD3) below, which a counterexample would have to satisfy, which has
no solution with $n \le 5000$ by direct computation, and all of whose
"$\ge 5$" solutions (exactly six pairs with $n\le 2000$) are killed by an
explicit $3$-power clause; a general proof that (LD3) is impossible is the
one missing step. Induction on $i$: a sharp **persistence lemma is proved**,
and the naive transfer is **refuted** by explicit small examples
($(16,2,5)$ and $(16,2,6)$), with an exact classification of when transfer
can fail.

Notation. $V_i(n)$ is the part of $\binom ni$ supported on primes $\ge i$;
the conjecture for the pair $(i,j)$ is $\gcd(V_i(n),\binom nj)>1$ for all
$i<j\le n/2$. "Failure at $(n,i,j)$" means $\gcd(V_i(n),\binom nj)=1$.
Two project inputs are used, both from `erdos-699/PHASE0.md`:

* **Key lemma.** $\binom ni\binom{\,n-i\,}{j-i}=\binom nj\binom ji$; hence if
  $d\mid\binom ni$ and $\gcd(d,\binom nj)=1$ then $d\mid\binom ji$.
* **Kummer/Lucas.** For a prime $p$, $v_p(\binom nj)\ge 1$ iff a carry
  occurs when adding $j$ and $n-j$ in base $p$, iff $j$ is **not**
  digitwise $\le n$ in base $p$.

---

## 1. Idea

For $i=1$ and $i=2$ the threshold "$p\ge i$" excludes *no* prime at all:
every prime is $\ge 1$, and every prime is $\ge 2$. Hence $V_1(n)=\binom n1$
and $V_2(n)=\binom n2$ — the *whole* binomial coefficient, not a part of it —
and the key lemma applied with $d=\binom ni$ itself closes both cases in
three lines, with no EEES78 input, no Kummer analysis, and no size
hypothesis. The first genuinely hard case is $i=3$, where $V_3(n)$ is the
*odd part* of $\binom n3$ and the factor $2$ is exactly the uncontrolled
prime. For $i=3$ the workhorse is a prime-power form of Kummer's criterion:
if $p^a$ exactly divides one of the top three numbers $n,n-1,n-2$, then the
lowest $a$ base-$p$ digits of $n$ are forced to be $(r,0,\dots,0)$ where
$r\in\{0,1,2\}$ is that number's offset, so $p\nmid\binom nj$ forces the
very restrictive congruence $j\bmod p^a\le r$. Collecting these congruences
over all relevant prime powers gives a system (LD3) that a counterexample
must satisfy; empirically the system is inconsistent in every range tested,
and the same lemma stated for general fixed $i$ covers, as a *sufficient*
criterion, every triple tested for $i\le 10$. Induction on $i$, by
contrast, is mostly a dead end: primes can leave (cancelled by the factor
$i+1$), primes can enter (through the factor $n-i$), and the threshold
itself moves; the examples in §5 show all three phenomena at once in a
single triple, and the exact statement that survives is a persistence
lemma plus a classification of the degenerate cases.

## 2. The cases $i=1$ and $i=2$: complete proofs

### 2.1. $i=1$ — complete

**Theorem 1.** For every $n$ and every $1<j\le n/2$,
$\gcd\!\left(n,\binom nj\right)>1$.

*Proof.* Suppose not: $\gcd(n,\binom nj)=1$. Apply the key lemma with
$d=\binom n1=n$ (which divides $\binom n1$ trivially): then $n\mid\binom j1=j$.
But $0<j\le n/2<n$, so $n\nmid j$, a contradiction. $\square$

Remark. This bypasses the digitwise analysis suggested in the assignment
(largest prime factor, $\mathrm{rad}(n)\mid j$, etc.): that analysis is
correct as far as it goes, but the key lemma gives the same conclusion —
indeed $n\mid j$ is exactly the "$\mathrm{rad}$-type" divisibility the
digit argument was heading for, obtained for the *whole* of $n$ at once.
No hard sub-case remains; in particular the feared configuration "$n$ a
product of small primes to high powers with $j$ a multiple of
$\mathrm{rad}(n)$" cannot occur under the failure hypothesis, because
failure forces $n\mid j$, not merely $\mathrm{rad}(n)\mid j$.

### 2.2. $i=2$ — complete

**Theorem 2.** For every $n$ and every $2<j\le n/2$,
$\gcd\!\left(\binom n2,\binom nj\right)>1$; in particular $\binom n2$ and
$\binom nj$ share a prime $\ge 2$ (i.e. any prime).

*Proof.* Suppose $\gcd(\binom n2,\binom nj)=1$. Apply the key lemma with
$d=\binom n2$: then $\binom n2\mid\binom j2$, so $\binom n2\le\binom j2$.
But $\binom x2=x(x-1)/2$ is strictly increasing for $x\ge 2$, and
$2\le j<n$, so $\binom j2<\binom n2$, a contradiction. $\square$

Note the proof needs no lower bound on the prime: since the required
threshold is $p\ge2$, *any* common prime works, and the proof shows the
stronger statement $\gcd>1$.

Why this stops at $i=2$: for $i\ge3$ the threshold $p\ge i$ discards at
least the prime $2$, so $V_i(n)$ is a proper divisor of $\binom ni$, the
key lemma applied to $V_i(n)$ only yields $V_i(n)\mid\binom ji$ and
$V_i(n)\le\binom ji$, and $V_i(n)$ can be far smaller than $\binom ni$
(e.g. $n=2^a$: $v_2(\binom n3)=a$, so $V_3(n)=\binom n3/n\approx n^2/6$,
while $\binom{j}{3}$ at $j\approx n/2$ is $\approx n^3/48$). Size alone
cannot close $i\ge3$.

## 3. The case $i=3$: reduction to the system (LD3)

Throughout, $3<j\le n/2$ (the range $j\le 3i/2$, i.e. $j=4$, is already
closed by the established $j\le 3i/2$ theorem; our arguments in fact cover
$j=4$ as well in most cases, but we state the target as the open region).

### 3.1. The prime-power low-digit lemma (the workhorse)

**Lemma 3 (low digits).** Let $q=p^a$ exactly divide $m=n-r$ for some
$r\in\{0,1,2\}$, where either (a) $p\ge5$, or (b) $p=3$ and $a\ge2$.
Then $p\mid\binom n3$, and
$$p\nmid\binom nj\ \Longrightarrow\ j\bmod q\ \le\ r.$$
Equivalently: **if $j\bmod q>r$ then $p$ is a common prime $\ge3$ of
$\binom n3$ and $\binom nj$, and the conjecture holds for $(n,3,j)$.**

*Proof.* First, $p\mid\binom n3$. For (a): $p\nmid 6$, and $p$ divides the
product $n(n-1)(n-2)$ (it divides $m$), while the denominator of
$\binom n3$ is $6$; so $v_p(\binom n3)=v_p(n(n-1)(n-2))\ge a\ge1$ ($p$
divides only one of the three factors, since two of them differ by
$\le2<p$). For (b): exactly one of three consecutive integers is divisible
by $3$, it is $m$, and $v_3(\binom n3)=a-v_3(6)=a-1\ge1$.

Second, the digit claim. Since $q\mid n-r$ and $0\le r<p$ (as $r\le2<3\le p$),
we have $n\equiv r\pmod q$, so the lowest $a$ base-$p$ digits of $n$ are
the base-$p$ expansion of $r$ padded to $a$ digits: digit $0$ is $r$ and
digits $1,\dots,a-1$ are $0$. If $p\nmid\binom nj$ then by Kummer $j$ is
digitwise $\le n$ in base $p$; hence $j$'s digit $0$ is $\le r$ and its
digits $1,\dots,a-1$ are $0$, i.e. $j\bmod q\le r$. $\square$

Two special cases worth naming:

* **Large prime ($a=1$, $q=p>j$).** Then $j\bmod p=j>r$ automatically, so
  *any* prime $p\ge5$ dividing $n(n-1)(n-2)$ with $p>j$ closes the triple.
  In particular if any of $n,n-1,n-2$ is itself prime (it exceeds $n/2\ge j$),
  the triple is closed. (Elementary restatement avoiding digits: $p$ divides
  one of the top $j$ factors of $\binom nj$'s numerator — the top three are
  among them as $j>3$ — and $p\nmid j!$ since $p>j$.)
* **Prime powers beat primes.** $q$ can exceed $j$ even when $p\le j$:
  e.g. $n=50$, $49=7^2\mid n-1$, $q=49$; for every $4\le j\le25$,
  $j\bmod 49=j>1=r$, so $7\mid\binom{50}{j}$ and $7\mid\binom{50}{3}$.
  The single-prime criterion $p>j$ misses all of these ($p=7\le j$).

### 3.2. The system (LD3)

Collect Lemma 3 over *all* relevant prime powers. For $m\in\{n,n-1,n-2\}$
let $R(m)$ be the product of the prime powers $q=p^a\Vert m$ with $p\ge5$
(any $a\ge1$) or $p=3$, $a\ge2$ (these are exactly the prime powers for
which Lemma 3 applies; note the factors are pairwise coprime, also
*across* the three numbers, since a prime $p\ge3$ — in fact $p>2$ — cannot
divide two of $n,n-1,n-2$). Failure at $(n,3,j)$ implies, for
$r=n-m\in\{0,1,2\}$ and each such $q\mid R(m)$:
$$j\bmod q\ \in\ \{0,1,\dots,r\},\qquad\text{i.e. } q\mid j,\ q\mid(j-1),\
\text{or } q\mid(j-2)\ \text{(only the first $r+1$ options allowed).}$$
In particular:
* $R(n)\mid j$ (the $r=0$ case: every relevant prime power of $n$ divides $j$);
* $R(n-1)=X_0X_1$ with $X_0\mid j$, $X_1\mid j-1$;
* $R(n-2)=Y_0Y_1Y_2$ with $Y_s\mid j-s$.

**Definition (LD3).** $(n,j)$ *satisfies (LD3)* if all of the displayed
congruence conditions hold for all relevant prime powers. By Lemma 3,
**a counterexample at level $i=3$ must satisfy (LD3).** (LD3) is a
necessary condition, not a sufficient one: it uses only the lowest
$a$ base-$p$ digits; a pair satisfying (LD3) could still be saved by
higher digits. So (LD3) is exactly the obstruction *for this method*, and
the exact remaining sub-case for $i=3$.

**Remaining sub-case $H_3$ (precise).** Prove that (LD3) has no solution
with $3<j\le n/2$ — equivalently, derive a contradiction from the system
above for arbitrary $n$ — or, failing that, characterise its solutions
and close them with the higher-digit Kummer conditions. Everything else
about $i=3$ in this section is proved unconditionally.

### 3.3. Two further necessary conditions a counterexample must meet

**(N1) The level-2 gcd is $6$-smooth.** Suppose failure at $(n,3,j)$. If a
prime $p\ge5$ divided both $\binom n2$ and $\binom nj$, then $p\mid n(n-1)$
($p$ odd, $p\nmid 2$), hence $p\mid\binom n3$ as in Lemma 3(a), so $p$
would be a level-3 witness — contradiction. Therefore
$$\gcd\!\left(\tbinom n2,\tbinom nj\right)=2^{a}3^{b}\ \text{for some }a,b\ge0.$$
(Converse direction, a proved transfer fragment: if
$\gcd(\binom n2,\binom nj)$ has *any* prime factor $\ge5$, then $(n,3,j)$
is closed, by the same observation. And if $3\mid\binom n3$ and
$3\mid\binom nj$ the triple is closed trivially.)

**(N2) Key-lemma bound.** Failure gives $\gcd(V_3(n),\binom nj)=1$ with
$V_3(n)\mid\binom n3$, so by the key lemma $V_3(n)\mid\binom j3$; in
particular $V_3(n)\le\binom j3$, where
$V_3(n)=U_0U_1U_2\cdot 3^{\,v_3(n(n-1)(n-2))-1}$ and $U_r$ is the part of
$n-r$ supported on primes $\ge5$.

### 3.4. Computational status of (LD3), and the six exceptional pairs

Direct computation (Python stdlib, exact prime-power factorisation of
$n,n-1,n-2$ and the test of Lemma 3's criterion $j\bmod q>r$ for some
relevant $q$):

* The criterion of Lemma 3 **fires for every one of the $6{,}235{,}009$
  triples** with $6\le n\le5000$, $4\le j\le n/2$. I.e. (LD3) has **no
  solution with $n\le5000$**: for $i=3$ the low-digit conditions alone
  are already inconsistent in this range (of course consistent with, and
  much weaker than, the project's sweep to $n\le10000$, but it shows the
  *method* loses nothing there).
* For comparison, the bare single-prime criterion $p>j$ (§3.1) fires for
  only $31{,}818$ of the $38{,}809$ triples with $n\le400$; the
  prime-power refinement is doing real work.
* **Dropping the $p=3$ clause** from Lemma 3, the $\ge5$ system has
  exactly **six** solutions with $n\le2000$:
  $(10,5)$, $(28,14)$, $(82,41)$, $(244,122)$, $(730,365)$ — all with
  $n=3^a+1$, $j=n/2$ — and $(162,70)$. Each is killed by the $3$-clause
  explicitly: for $n=3^a+1$ ($a\ge2$), $q=3^a\mid n-1$ ($r=1$) and
  $j\bmod 3^a=j=(3^a+1)/2>1$, so $3\mid\binom nj$ and $3\mid\binom n3$
  (indeed this *proves* the whole family $n=3^a+1$, $j=n/2$, $a\ge2$ at
  level $i=3$); for $(162,70)$, $q=81\mid162$ ($r=0$) and
  $70\bmod 81=70>0$, so again $3$ is the witness.

### 3.5. An odd-top-number reduction (proved, not yet a closure)

Among $n,n-1,n-2$ at least one number $m$ is odd. For odd $m$ the
relevant part is rigid: $R(m)=m$ if $3\nmid m$ or $9\mid m$, and
$R(m)=m/3$ if $v_3(m)=1$. Hence under (LD3):

* if $n$ is odd and $R(n)=n$ ($3\nmid n$ or $9\mid n$), then $n\mid j$:
  impossible. So (LD3) with $n$ odd forces $v_3(n)=1$, $R(n)=n/3\mid j$,
  and $j\le n/2$ then pins $j=n/3$ **exactly**;
* if $n$ is even, the odd number is $n-1$ ($r=1$): $R(n-1)\in\{n-1,(n-1)/3\}$
  must split as $X_0X_1$ with $X_0\mid j$, $X_1\mid j-1$, both
  $X_0,X_1\le j\le n/2$; in particular if $R(n-1)$ is a single prime
  power exceeding $n/2$ (the case $R(n-1)=n-1$, e.g. $n-1$ prime) it can
  divide neither $j$ nor $j-1$, and the triple is closed, matching §3.1.

This reduces a putative proof of $H_3$ to a short list of rigid patterns
($n$ odd $\Rightarrow j=n/3$ pinned; $n$ even $\Rightarrow$ a splitting
condition on $n-1$ plus the analogous $r=2$ condition on the $6$-part of
$n-2$), but the splitting cases with $R(m)$ having two or more prime
power factors have resisted a uniform size argument: the factors can be
distributed among $j,j-1,j-2$, each individually $\le j$, and no bound
we found excludes all distributions simultaneously. **This is the exact
place the $i=3$ proof currently stops.**

## 4. (Cross-reference) What §3 proves unconditionally for $i=3$

For any *specific* triple $(n,3,j)$ (any $n$, including $n>10000$), the
conjecture is **proved** as soon as any one of these checkable conditions
holds: (i) some prime $p\ge5$ dividing $n(n-1)(n-2)$ has $p>j$;
(ii) some relevant prime power $q$ (Lemma 3) has $j\bmod q>r$;
(iii) $\gcd(\binom n2,\binom nj)$ has a prime factor $\ge5$;
(iv) $3\mid\binom n3$ and $3\mid\binom nj$. Condition (ii) subsumes (i).
Infinite families covered for all $n$ include: any of $n,n-1,n-2$ prime;
$n=3^a+1$ with $j=n/2$ ($a\ge2$); $n=50$, all $j$ (via $q=49$).

## 5. Sub-route (B): induction on $i$

### 5.1. The persistence lemma (proved)

From $\binom{n}{i+1}=\binom ni\cdot\frac{n-i}{i+1}$,
$$v_p\!\left(\tbinom{n}{i+1}\right)=v_p\!\left(\tbinom ni\right)+v_p(n-i)-v_p(i+1)
\qquad\text{for every prime }p.$$

**Lemma 4 (persistence).** If $p\ge i+1$, $p\nmid i+1$, and
$p\mid\binom ni$ and $p\mid\binom nj$, then $p\mid\binom{n}{i+1}$; i.e.
the *same* prime witnesses level $i+1$ for the *same* $(n,j)$.

*Proof.* $v_p(\binom{n}{i+1})\ge v_p(\binom ni)\ge1$ since
$v_p(n-i)\ge0$ and $v_p(i+1)=0$. $\square$

**Corollary (exact transfer-failure classification).** Suppose level $i$
holds for $(n,j)$ (with $j>i+1$) but level $i+1$ *fails* for $(n,j)$.
Then every prime $p\ge i$ dividing both $\binom ni$ and $\binom nj$ is of
one of two types: **(threshold)** $p=i$; or **(cancellation)** $i+1$ is
prime, $p=i+1$, and the cancellation is exact:
$v_p(\binom ni)+v_p(n-i)=v_p(i+1)=1$, so $v_p(\binom{n}{i+1})=0$.
(For $i>1$ these are the only possibilities: a prime divisor of $i+1$
that is $\ge i$ must equal $i+1$.) Additionally, any prime $\ge i+1$
dividing $\binom{n}{i+1}$ but not $\binom ni$ (an *entering* prime, via
$p\mid n-i$) is invisible to level $i$.

So induction can be repaired only in the form: *level $i$ for $(n,j)$,
plus the extra hypothesis that its witness is not confined to the
degenerate set $\{i\}\cup\{\text{cancelling }i+1\}$, implies level
$i+1$ for $(n,j)$.* Section 3.3 (N1) is exactly this repair used at
$i=2\to3$: there the degenerate set is $\{2\}\cup\{3\text{ cancelling}\}$,
and (N1) says a level-3 counterexample forces the level-2 gcd into
$2^a3^b$.

### 5.2. The naive transfer is false: explicit examples

**Example A (all three obstructions in one triple).** $(n,i,j)=(16,2,5)$:
$$\tbinom{16}2=120,\quad \tbinom{16}5=4368,\quad \gcd=24=2^3\cdot3,$$
so the level-$2$ common primes are exactly $\{2,3\}$. Both are useless
at level $3$: $2<3$ (threshold), and $3\mid i+1=3$ cancels exactly:
$v_3(\binom{16}2)=1$, $v_3(16-2)=v_3(14)=0$, sum $=1=v_3(3)$, so
$3\nmid\binom{16}3=560$. Level $3$ nevertheless holds, via an *entering*
prime invisible to level $2$:
$\gcd(\binom{16}3,\binom{16}5)=\gcd(560,4368)=112=2^4\cdot7$, and
$7\mid 16-2=14$ is the factor that enters through $(n-i)/(i+1)=14/3$.

**Example B.** $(n,i,j)=(16,2,6)$: $\gcd(\binom{16}2,\binom{16}6)=
\gcd(120,8008)=8$ — the level-$2$ statement's *only* witness is the
prime $2$, below the level-$3$ threshold — while
$\gcd(\binom{16}3,\binom{16}6)=56$, witness $7$, again entering.

**Example C (a prime leaving, in isolation).** $3\mid\binom{13}2=78$ but
$3\nmid\binom{13}3=286$ ($v_3: 1+v_3(11)-v_3(3)=0$); simultaneously
$11\mid\binom{13}3$ enters from $n-i=11$.

**Verdict for (B).** There is no transfer lemma of the form "level $i$
(for $(n,j)$, or for any $(n,j')$ — a different $j'$ controls
$\binom n{j'}$, not $\binom nj$) implies level $i+1$". A counterexample at
level $i+1$ does **not** yield one at level $i$, and a proof at level $i$
yields level $i+1$ only through Lemma 4 plus a separate argument ruling
out the degenerate witness configurations of the Corollary. Induction is
therefore a *bookkeeping* tool (it produced (N1) and the $i=2\to3$
reduction), not an engine.

## 6. Honest assessment

**What is newly settled, exactly.** Unconditionally, for **all** $n$:

* $i=1$: every pair $(1,j)$, $j\ge2$, every $n\ge2j$. Previously (RESULTS.md)
  the open-region pairs $(1,j)$ were fully cleared only for $j\le J_1=70$;
  **all $(1,j)$ with $j>70$ are new**, as is an elementary re-proof for
  $j\le70$.
* $i=2$: every pair $(2,j)$, $j\ge3$, every $n\ge2j$. Previously fully
  cleared only for $j\le J_2=93$ in the open region ($j=3$ was in the
  closed region); **all $(2,j)$ with $j>93$ are new**.
* $i=3$: **not** settled in general. Settled: every specific triple
  satisfying any condition in §4 — in particular all triples in the
  families listed there, for arbitrarily large $n$ (the sweep cannot
  reach $n>10000$ at all, so e.g. $(3,j,n)$ with $n-1$ prime and
  $n>10000$ is new). A full $i=3$ closure is exactly equivalent, *for
  this route*, to proving $H_3$ (§3.2/§3.5); it would settle all
  $(3,j)$, $j\ge4$, for all $n$ (previously $j\le J_3=112$).

**Complementarity with the sweep.** The sweep clears a pair $(i,j)$ by
bounding $n\le N(i,j)$; for fixed $i$ the bound $N(i,j)$ grows with $j$,
so a fixed-$i$, all-$j$ proof is orthogonal to it: it imposes
*factorisation constraints linking $n$ and $j$* that hold uniformly in
$n$, and it is precisely the $n>10000$ tail — and, for $i=1,2$, the
large-$j$ pairs the sweep's pair classification never reaches — that it
removes. Conversely the fixed-$i$ method says nothing for large $i$.

**How far can "small $i$" be pushed?** Lemma 3 generalises verbatim to
fixed $i$: for a prime $p>i$ and $q=p^a$ exactly dividing $n-r$
($0\le r<i$), $p\mid\binom ni$ and $p\nmid\binom nj$ imply $j\bmod q\le r$
(same digit proof; and for prime $i=p$ the clause $a\ge2$ gives
$p\mid\binom ni$ since $v_p(i!)=1$). Empirically the resulting sufficient
criterion fires for **every** triple tested beyond $i=3$: all triples for
$i=4$ and $i=5$ with $n\le600$ ($87{,}616$ and $87{,}025$ triples), and
for $i=6,7,8,9,10$ with $n\le300$ ($\approx20{,}000$ triples each) — zero
survivors. This makes $i\le5$ a plausible target for the same programme:
reduce to an (LD$i$) system, then attack the system by the odd-top-number
rigidity of §3.5 generalised to "the top-$i$ number coprime to $i!$-small
primes". Two honest caveats. First, the (LD$i$) systems are necessary,
not sufficient, so their inconsistency must be *proved*, and already at
$i=3$ the splitting sub-case (§3.5) resists the size arguments we have;
the difficulty is a CRT/distribution problem (prime powers of one top
number distributing among $j,j-1,\dots,j-r$), whose complexity grows
with $i$. Second, for $i\ge6$ the number of residue patterns and
small-prime configurations makes a hand case analysis implausible
without a new idea; a realistic expectation is: $i=4,5$ attainable with
substantial further work along these lines, $i\le10$ supported only as
computational evidence, and no fixed-$i$ method of this shape touches
the regime where both $i$ and $j$ grow, which remains the province of
the EEES78/key-lemma reduction and the sweep.

**Computation used.** All counts and examples above were verified with
Python stdlib exact arithmetic in under a minute each: brute-force
$\gcd$ checks for $i=1,2,3$ to $n\le500$ (zero failures); the Lemma 3
criterion to $n\le5000$ for $i=3$ (and its $\ge5$-only variant to
$n\le2000$); the
general-$i$ criterion for $i=4,\dots,10$ in the ranges stated; and the
specific gcds in §5.2.
