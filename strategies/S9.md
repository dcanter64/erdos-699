# S9 - Analytic number theory: largest prime factors and the anatomy of C(n,i)

Agent: S9 (Cycle 1, strategy round). Status: strategy paper, not a proof.
Problem: for $1 \le i < j \le n/2$, some prime $p \ge i$ divides both
$C(n,i)$ and $C(n,j)$. Notation: $P^+(m)$ is the largest prime factor of
$m$, $v = V_i(n)$ is the part of $C(n,i)$ on primes $\ge i$, $\log_2 x =
\log\log x$ and $\log_3 x = \log\log\log x$ (iterated logs, following the
surveys cited below). Established inputs from the project: the key lemma
($d \mid C(n,i)$, $\gcd(d, C(n,j)) = 1$ implies $d \mid C(j, j-i)$), the
EEES78 bound $v > C(n,i)^{1/2}$ off 12 explicit pairs, the closure for
$j \le 3i/2$, and a clean sweep for $n \le 10000$ in the open region.

## 1. Idea

The cheapest sufficient condition for the conjecture is a prime that is
too large to be avoided: if $P^+(C(n,i)) > j$, that prime automatically
divides $C(n,j)$ as well (Lemma A below), so the whole question becomes
how large a prime factor the anatomy of $C(n,i)$ forces. A century of
work on the Sylvester-Schur theorem says $C(n,i)$ always has a prime
factor exceeding $i$, and the modern refinements push this to $1.97i$,
$2i$, conditionally $4.42i$, and asymptotically to about $i \log i$ times
an iterated-log factor, with the strength growing when $n$ is large
compared with $i$. Each such bound $P^+(C(n,i)) > B(i,n)$ closes, in one
stroke and for every $n$ in its range, all $j \le B(i,n)$, so this route
converts the open region $j > 3i/2$ into $j > B(i,n)$ and is strongest
exactly near the diagonal. Supplementing the largest prime factor with
the number $\omega(C(n,i))$ of distinct prime factors gives more: in a
counterexample every prime power $p^a \| v$ divides $C(j, j-i)$, so the
product of the distinct large primes of $C(n,i)$ is at most $C(j, j-i)
\le 2^j$, and known lower bounds for $\omega(C(n,i))$ then close
$j \lesssim \frac{1}{\log 2} i \log i$ with an explicit constant in the
range $n \ge i\pi(i) + i$. At the other end, when $i$ is large compared
with $n$, prime-gap theorems (Dusart explicitly, Baker-Harman-Pintz
asymptotically) put a prime in the top block $(n-i, n]$ itself, and such
a prime exceeds $j$ automatically, closing all $j$ at once. Finally, a
counterexample has a clean equivalent form (Section 5): every prime
$p > j$ satisfies $n \bmod p \ge i$, which says simultaneously that the
$i$ consecutive integers $n-i+1, \dots, n$ are all $j$-smooth and that
the interval $((n-i)/q,\ n/q]$ is prime free for every $q \ge 1$. The
route dies, provably, when $i$ is small and $j$ is comparable to $n/2$:
then $P^+(C(n,i)) \le j$ happens for genuine infinite families
(Section 6), and no bound depending only on the anatomy of $C(n,i)$ can
rescue it.

## 2. Two elementary lemmas and the transfer principle

**Lemma A (transfer for $p > j$).** Let $p > j$ be prime with
$p \mid C(n,i)$. Then $p \mid C(n,j)$.
Proof 1 (ratio). $C(n,j) = C(n,i) \cdot R$ with
$R = \prod_{s=i+1}^{j} \frac{n-s+1}{s}$. Every denominator factor
$s \le j < p$, so $v_p$ of the denominator product is $0$; the numerator
product is an integer, so $v_p(R) \ge 0$ and
$v_p(C(n,j)) = v_p(C(n,i)) + v_p(R) \ge 1$. $\square$
Proof 2 (key lemma). If $p \nmid C(n,j)$ then $p \mid C(j, j-i)$ by the
key lemma, impossible since every prime divisor of $C(j, j-i)$ is
$\le j < p$. $\square$
Corollary: $P^+(C(n,i)) > j$ implies the conjecture for $(n,i,j)$.

**Lemma B (one-digit Kummer criterion).** Let $p > i$ be prime,
$p \le n$. Then $p \mid C(n,i)$ if and only if $n \bmod p < i$.
Proof. By Kummer's theorem $v_p(C(n,i))$ is the number of carries when
adding $i$ and $n-i$ in base $p$. Since $i < p$, the base-$p$ expansion
of $i$ is the single digit $i$. A carry occurs if and only if the units
digit of $n-i$ plus $i$ reaches $p$, i.e. if and only if the units digit
of $n$, which is $n \bmod p$, is $< i$ (then $n - i$ has units digit
$n \bmod p + p - i$ and the addition carries); with no units carry there
is no higher carry, since the higher digits of $i$ are $0$. (Equivalently,
the first Legendre term $\lfloor n/p\rfloor - \lfloor (n-i)/p\rfloor$
is $1$ iff $n \bmod p < i$, and it already decides divisibility in the
"if" direction, while Kummer gives the "only if".) $\square$

**Prime-power form of the key lemma (as assigned, checked).** In a
counterexample, for every prime $p \ge i$ with $p^a \| v$ we have
$p \nmid C(n,j)$, hence $\gcd(p^a, C(n,j)) = 1$ and the key lemma gives
$p^a \mid C(j, j-i)$. This is *equivalent* to $v \mid C(j, j-i)$ (the
$p^a$ for distinct $p$ are pairwise coprime, so they all divide
$C(j,j-i)$ iff their product does), but the prime-power form is the one
that interacts with anatomy: it gives $a \le v_p(C(j, j-i))$ prime by
prime, and in particular, taking only the distinct primes,
$\prod_{p \ge i,\, p \mid C(n,i)} p \le v \le C(j, j-i) \le 2^j$.
Section 4, is exactly this inequality fed with a lower bound for
the number of distinct prime factors. Note the hypothesis matters:
outside a counterexample the conclusion fails, e.g. $V_3(50) = 5^2
\cdot 7^2 = 1225 \nmid C(25, 22) = 2300$ (computed; $(50,3,25)$ is not
a counterexample because $7$ divides both binomials).

## 3. Known theorems relied on, with verification status

Translation used throughout: $C(n,i) = \Delta(x, i) / i!$ where
$x = n - i + 1$ and $\Delta(x, i) = x(x+1)\cdots(x+i-1)$ is a product of
$i$ consecutive integers. A prime $p > i$ divides $\Delta(x,i)$ iff it
divides $C(n,i)$ (it cannot divide $i!$), so $P^+(C(n,i)) = P(\Delta(x,
i))$ whenever the latter exceeds $i$, which all results below
guarantee. Block results quoted with start $x$ and length $k$ are
applied with $x = n-i+1$, $k = i$.

- **T1 (Sylvester 1892; Schur 1929; Erdős 1934).** If $n \ge 2k$ then
  $C(n,k)$ has a prime factor $> k$. Sylvester, "On arithmetical
  series", Messenger of Math. 21 (1892); Schur (1929); elementary proof:
  Erdős, "A theorem of Sylvester and Schur", J. London Math. Soc. 9
  (1934), 282-288. VERIFIED (statement and Erdős proof located; also
  the form "one of $n, n-1, \dots, n-k+1$ has a prime divisor $> k$" in
  Aigner-Ziegler, *Proofs from THE BOOK*).
- **T2 (Hanson 1973).** $P(\Delta(x,k)) > 1.5k$ unless
  $(x,k) \in \{(3,2), (8,2), (6,5)\}$. Hanson, Canad. Math. Bull. 16
  (1973), 195-199. VERIFIED as reported in Laishram-Shorey (below).
  Not used in the main chain (superseded by T3).
- **T3 (Laishram-Shorey 2005), the workhorse.** Laishram and Shorey,
  "The greatest prime divisor of a product of consecutive integers",
  Acta Arith. 120.3 (2005), 299-310, Theorem 1: for $\Delta(x,k)$,
  (a) $P(\Delta(x,k)) > 2k$ for $x > \max(k + 13,\ 279k/262)$;
  (b) $P(\Delta(x,k)) > 1.97k$ for $x > k + 13$.
  Also (survey form) $P > 1.95k$ for $x > k$ except for an explicitly
  given finite set of pairs, with no exceptions when $k > 270$ or
  $x > k + 11$. VERIFIED from the primary paper (Theorem 1 statement)
  and from Shorey-Tijdeman's survey §1.1. CAUTION: the same survey's
  §1.4 prints "$P > 1.95k$ for all $n \ge 2k$" without the exception
  clause; I do *not* use that form. Only (a) and (b), which are stated
  without exceptions, enter the main chain.
- **T4 (Nair-Shorey, FLAGGED).** As reported in Shorey's survey
  "Arithmetic properties of blocks of consecutive integers"
  (arXiv:1612.05438), §3.1: $P(\Delta(x,k)) > 4.42k$ for $x > 4k$, and
  $> 4.5k$ for $x > 4k$ and $k \ne 82, 83$. FLAGGED, NOT VERIFIED from
  the original: a secondary summary of Nair-Shorey states the $4.42k$
  conclusion "except only finitely many pairs $(x,k)$, which they
  determined explicitly". The two reports conflict on whether an
  exception list exists. PC2 below is therefore conditional on
  importing Nair-Shorey's original exception list (expected to be
  finite and explicit, hence dischargeable like the EEES78 list).
- **T5 (asymptotic $P^+$ bounds; constants implicit, FLAGGED as to
  thresholds).** From arXiv:1612.05438, §3.2, for $\Delta(x,k)$:
  (i) Jia and Liu: for $x < k^{2-\varepsilon}$, $0 < \varepsilon \le 0.1$,
  $P(\Delta) \gg_\varepsilon k^{25/13 - \varepsilon}$;
  (ii) Shorey: $P(\Delta) \gg k \log k \cdot \frac{\log_2 k}{\log_3 k}$,
  and "combining the above results we see that (this bound) is valid
  for $x > k^{3/2}$";
  (iii) Shorey-Tijdeman: for $x > \exp_2(\log^2 k / \log_2 k)$,
  $P(\Delta) \gg k \log_2 x$.
  VERIFIED as statements in the survey; the implied constants and the
  "sufficiently large" thresholds are not made explicit there, so
  consequences (PC3) are stated with unspecified constants $c_0, i_0$.
  These come from linear forms in logarithms and are in principle
  effective, but no effective values are claimed here.
- **T6 (Shorey-Tijdeman $\omega$ theorem).** Shorey and Tijdeman,
  "Prime factors of arithmetic progressions and binomial
  coefficients" (survey, Leiden PDF), §1.4, Theorem 1: for $N > 2k$,
  (i) $\omega(C(N,k)) \ge k - \frac{\log(k!)}{\log(N-k)}$;
  (ii) if $N \ge k\pi(k) + k$ then $\omega(C(N,k)) \ge k$.
  VERIFIED from the survey text, including its self-contained proof
  sketch (divide $k!$ out of the $k$ numerator terms one prime at a
  time; a term reducing to $1$ costs a factor $> N - k$, and at most
  $\log(k!)/\log(N-k)$ terms can do so).
- **T7 (EEES78, project-established).** $v > C(n,i)^{1/2}$ except for
  the 12 listed pairs. Ecklund, Eggleton, Erdős, Selfridge, J. Austral.
  Math. Soc. A 26 (1978), 257-269. Used via PHASE0.md.
- **T8 (Mahler 1961, context only, secondhand).** As quoted in the
  introduction of EEES78: for fixed $a > 0$ and $k$, the part of
  $C(n,k)$ composed of primes $\le k$ is $< n^{1+a}$ for $n$
  sufficiently large (threshold ineffective). This is the historical
  predecessor of T7. FLAGGED: quoted secondhand, not from Mahler's
  original; used for context only, never in a derivation.
- **T9 (prime gaps).** (a) Baker, Harman, Pintz, "The difference
  between consecutive primes II", Proc. London Math. Soc. 83 (2001),
  532-562: for all sufficiently large $x$ there is a prime in
  $[x, x + x^{0.525}]$, indeed $\gg x^{0.525}/\log x$ of them.
  (b) Dusart (explicit): for $x \ge 396738$ there is a prime in
  $(x, x(1 + \frac{1}{25\log^2 x})]$ (2010, Prop. 6.8), and for
  $x \ge 89693$ there is a prime in $(x, x(1 + \frac{1}{\log^3 x})]$
  (2016, Prop. 5.4). VERIFIED: (a) from the Shorey-Tijdeman survey's
  reference and statement ($C = 0.525$ "best result up to now" there)
  and multiple secondary sources; (b) from secondary sources
  (Bertrand's postulate reference summary and an explicit-intervals
  review table). FLAGGED: (b) was verified from secondary sources, not
  from Dusart's papers directly; logs are natural.
- **T10 (Jacobsthal, analogy only).** Iwaniec (1978): the Jacobsthal
  function satisfies $j(N) \ll (\log N)^2$. VERIFIED secondhand
  (quoted as "Theorem B" in Kalmynin-Konyagin, Izv. Math. 88 (2024)).
  Used only as an analogy in Section 5; it does not apply directly,
  for reasons stated there.

**A tempting statement that is FALSE (do not use).** "Bertrand's
postulate gives a prime in $(i, 2i]$, and it divides $C(n,i)$." The
prime in $(i, 2i]$ need not divide $C(n,i)$: $C(12,5) = 792 =
2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 11$, and the prime $7 \in (5, 10]$ does not divide
it (computed). What is true is only that *some* prime $> i$ (possibly
composite-powered, possibly $> 2i$) divides $C(n,i)$, namely T1. Any
lemma in this campaign of the form "the prime in $(i,2i]$ works" is
invalid.

## 4. Derived partial closures

All statements are of the form: under the stated hypotheses there is
no counterexample $(n,i,j)$; equivalently the conjecture holds there.
The already closed region $j \le 3i/2$ is not restated except where a
statement subsumes part of it. Throughout, $x = n - i + 1$ is the block
start, so $x > i + 13$ iff $n \ge 2i + 14$, and $x > 4i$ iff
$n \ge 5i$.

**PC1 (constant factor $1.97$, clean; $2$ with a side condition).**
If $n \ge 2i + 14$ and $j \le 1.97\,i$, there is no counterexample.
More precisely, if $x > \max(i + 13,\ 279i/262)$ and $j \le 2i$, there
is no counterexample.
Proof. By T3(b) (resp. T3(a)) applied to $\Delta(x, i)$,
$P^+(C(n,i)) > 1.97\,i \ge j$ (resp. $> 2i \ge j$); Lemma A finishes.
$\square$
Remarks. (i) The side condition in the $2i$ form is $n \ge 2i + 14$
when $i \le 200$ (since $279i/262 \le i + 13$ iff $i \le 200.6$) and
$n > \frac{541}{262} i - 1$ when $i > 200$. (ii) The leftover of PC1
inside the band $j \le 1.97 i$ is $n \le 2i + 13$ with $n \ge 2j >
3i$, which forces $j \le i + 6$ and $i < 12$: a finite set of small
$i$, already inside the $n \le 10000$ sweep. Hence, combined with the
$j \le 3i/2$ closure, **for every $i \ge 12$ the full band
$j \le 1.97\,i$ is closed for all $n$**, with no computation beyond
the T3 exception discussion (T3(a),(b) have no exceptions).

**PC2 (constant factor $4.42$, CONDITIONAL on the T4 flag).**
If the Nair-Shorey bound T4 holds as stated in the survey (no
exceptions), then: if $n \ge 5i$ and $j \le 4.42\,i$, there is no
counterexample. If T4 instead has its explicitly determined finite
exception list (the conflicting secondary report), the same statement
holds outside that list, and the list is a finite discharge task of
exactly EEES78 type. Until the original is imported, treat PC2 as
conditional. Note its real content: since $j \le n/2$, for
$n < 5i$ the band $j \le 4.42 i$ is mostly inside $j \le 2.5 i$
anyway; PC2 earns its keep for $n \ge 5i$ and $j$ up to $4.42 i$,
well beyond what the reduction's $N(i,j)$ makes cheap when $i$ is
large.

**PC3 (asymptotic superlinear closure; the route's headline).**
There exist constants $c_0 > 0$ and $i_0$ (from T5, not explicit)
such that for all $i \ge i_0$, all $n \ge 2i$, and all
$$j \;\le\; c_0 \cdot i \log i \cdot \frac{\log_2 i}{\log_3 i},$$
there is no counterexample.
Proof sketch. Split on the block start $x = n - i + 1$.
If $x > i^{3/2}$, T5(ii) gives $P^+(C(n,i)) \gg i \log i \cdot
\frac{\log_2 i}{\log_3 i}$, which exceeds the displayed bound for
suitable $c_0$ and large $i$. If $x \le i^{3/2}$, then
$x < i^{2 - 0.1}$ and T5(i) with $\varepsilon = 0.1$ gives
$P^+(C(n,i)) \gg i^{25/13 - 0.1} = i^{1.823\ldots}$, which is
stronger than the displayed bound for large $i$. Lemma A finishes
in both cases. $\square$
Two strengthenings in special $n$ ranges, same source: in the
Jia-Liu range $x < i^{2-\varepsilon}$ alone, the closure reaches
$j \le c(\varepsilon)\, i^{25/13 - \varepsilon}$; and in the huge-$n$
range $x > \exp_2(\log^2 i / \log_2 i)$, T5(iii) gives closure up to
$j \le c\, i \log_2 n$, useful when $\log_2 n \gg \log i$.

**PC4 (top block; explicit, closes all $j$ at once).**
(a) (Dusart form, explicit.) If $n - i \ge 89693$ and
$i \ge \frac{n - i}{\log^3 (n - i)}$, there is no counterexample for
any $j$.
(b) (Baker-Harman-Pintz form, asymptotic.) There exists $n_0$ such
that if $n \ge n_0$ and $i > n^{0.525}$, there is no counterexample
for any $j$.
Proof. (a) Apply T9(b) with $x = n - i \ge 89693$: there is a prime
$p \in (n - i,\ (n-i)(1 + 1/\log^3(n-i))]$, and the hypothesis
$i \ge (n-i)/\log^3(n-i)$ says the right endpoint is $\le n$, so
$p \in (n - i, n]$. Then $p > n - i \ge n/2 \ge j$ (using
$i \le n/2$), and $p \mid C(n,i)$ because $p$ is one of the numerator
terms $n - i + 1, \dots, n$ and $p > i$ so $p \nmid i!$. Lemma A
finishes. (b) Apply T9(a) with $x = n - n^{0.525}$ (large, since
$n \ge n_0$): a prime lies in $[x, x + x^{0.525}] \subseteq
[n - n^{0.525}, n]$, and $i > n^{0.525}$ puts it in $(n - i, n]$;
conclude as in (a). $\square$
Note PC4 is exactly the $q = 1$ case of the covering formulation in
Section 5, and it is the only case prime gaps alone can reach
(Section 5, item (iii)).

**PC5 ($\omega$ plus prime powers; auxiliary, explicit constant).**
(i) If $n \ge i\,\pi(i) + i$ and
$j < \frac{(i - \pi(i-1)) \log i}{\log 2}$, there is no
counterexample.
(ii) More generally, if $n > 2i$ and, with
$m(n,i) := i - \frac{\log(i!)}{\log(n - i)} - \pi(i-1)$, one has
$m(n,i) > 0$ and $j < \frac{m(n,i) \log i}{\log 2}$, there is no
counterexample.
Proof. Suppose a counterexample. The distinct primes $\ge i$
dividing $C(n,i)$ number at least $\omega(C(n,i)) - \pi(i-1)$.
By T6(ii) (resp. T6(i)), $\omega(C(n,i)) \ge i$ (resp.
$\ge i - \log(i!)/\log(n-i)$), so there are at least $i - \pi(i-1)$
(resp. $m(n,i)$) such primes. Each is $\ge i$, so their product is
$\ge i^{m}$ with $m$ the count; but by the prime-power form of the
key lemma (Section 2) their product divides, hence is at most,
$C(j, j-i) \le 2^j$. Taking logs, $m \log i \le j \log 2$,
contradicting the hypothesis on $j$. $\square$
Asymptotics: $\pi(i-1) = o(i)$, so (i) closes
$j \lesssim \frac{1}{\log 2} i \log i \approx 1.4427\, i \log i$,
with a fully explicit constant, in the range $n \ge i\pi(i) + i
\sim i^2 / \log i$. This beats PC3's unspecified $c_0$ and extra
iterated-log loss *inside its $n$ range*; PC3 remains the only
superlinear statement covering all $n$. In range (ii), for
$n \ge i^{1+\delta}$ one has $\log(i!) < i \log i$ hence
$m(n,i) \ge i - \frac{i}{1 + \delta} - \pi(i-1) =
\frac{\delta}{1+\delta} i - o(i)$, closing
$j \lesssim \frac{\delta}{(1+\delta)\log 2} i \log i$ there.
PC5 is flagged as auxiliary: it is an $\omega$ (anatomy) argument
rather than a pure $P^+$ argument, and may overlap another agent's
assigned route; the $P^+$ statements PC1 to PC4 stand alone.

**Summary of what S9 closes (union, open region $j > 3i/2$).**
$j \le 1.97 i$ for all large $n$ and in fact all $n$ when $i \ge 12$
(PC1); $j \le 2i$ under the stated side condition (PC1);
$j \le 4.42 i$ for $n \ge 5i$, conditionally (PC2);
$j \le c_0 i \log i \frac{\log_2 i}{\log_3 i}$ for $i \ge i_0$, all
$n$ (PC3); $j \lesssim 1.4427\, i \log i$ explicitly for
$n \ge i\pi(i) + i$ (PC5); and *every* $j$ when $i$ is within a
$\log^3$ factor, resp. a $0.525$ power, of $n$ (PC4). Everything
beyond these curves is untouched by this route; Section 6 says
exactly where and why.

## 5. The covering constraint: formulation and analysis

**Formulation.** For a triple $1 \le i < j \le n/2$, consider:
(a) $(n,i,j)$ is a counterexample;
(b) every prime $p$ with $j < p \le n$ satisfies $n \bmod p \ge i$
    (equivalently $n \bmod p \in [i, p)$);
(c) every one of the $i$ consecutive integers $n - i + 1, \dots, n$
    is $j$-smooth (has no prime factor $> j$);
(d) for every integer $q \ge 1$, the interval
    $\big(\frac{n-i}{q}, \frac{n}{q}\big]$ contains no prime $> j$.
Then (b), (c) and (d) are equivalent to each other, and (a) implies
them. They are the "$p > j$ part" of being a counterexample: (a) is
(b) *plus* the separate condition that no prime in $[i, j]$ divides
both binomials, which this route does not address. I use (b), (c),
(d) as necessary conditions a counterexample must satisfy, not as a
full characterization.
Proof. (a) implies (b): if $p > j$ and
$p \mid C(n,i)$ then $p \mid C(n,j)$ by Lemma A, so a counterexample
forces $p \nmid C(n,i)$ for all $p > j$, which by Lemma B (applicable
since $p > j > i$) is exactly $n \bmod p \ge i$.
(b) iff (c): a prime $p > j$ divides some term of the top block iff
$p \mid \Delta(x, i)$ iff (as $p > i$) $p \mid C(n,i)$ iff
$n \bmod p < i$ by Lemma B. (b) iff (d): given $p > j$ put
$q = \lfloor n/p \rfloor$; then $p \in (n/(q+1), n/q]$ and
$n \bmod p = n - qp < i$ iff $qp > n - i$ iff $p > (n-i)/q$, i.e.
iff $p \in \big(\frac{n-i}{q}, \frac{n}{q}\big]$. Conversely if a
prime $p > j$ lies in $\big(\frac{n-i}{q}, \frac{n}{q}\big]$ for
some $q$, then $qp \in (n - i, n]$ and
$(q+1)p = qp + p > (n - i) + i = n$ (using $p > j > i$), so
$\lfloor n/p \rfloor = q$ and the same computation applies.
$\square$

So a counterexample forces a *stack* of prime-free intervals, one at
every scale $n/q$, each of length $i/q$, i.e. each of *relative*
length $i/n$. Special cases: $q = 1$ says $(n - i, n]$ is prime
free (a prime gap of length $i$ sitting at the top of $[1,n]$);
$q = 2$ says $\big(\frac{n-i}{2}, \frac{n}{2}\big]$ is prime free;
and so on down to $q \approx n/j$, below which the intervals lie
under $j$ and the condition is vacuous.

**Analysis.**

(i) *Prime gaps kill $q = 1$, and only $q = 1$.* A prime-gap theorem
of the form "every interval $(x, x + x^\theta]$ (or $(x, x(1+\delta
(x))]$) with $x$ large contains a prime" contradicts the $q$-th
interval only if the guaranteed subinterval fits inside it, i.e.
only if $i/q \gtrsim ((n-i)/q)^\theta$, i.e. $i \gtrsim
q^{1-\theta} n^\theta$ up to constants. The right side *grows* with
$q$ (for the Dusart form: one needs $i \ge (n - i)/\log^3((n-i)/q)$,
and $(n-i)/\log^3(\cdot)$ grows as the scale drops). Optimizing over
$q$ therefore always selects $q = 1$: PC4 is the complete output of
the prime-gap input on this formulation. Deeper scales need a
different idea, not a better gap theorem.

(ii) *Why Jacobsthal does not apply.* Condition (c) looks like a
covering system: the residues $n \bmod p \in [i, p)$ say that the
block of length $i$ ending at $n$ avoids the residue $0$ modulo
every $p > j$. Jacobsthal's function $j(N) \ll (\log N)^2$ (T10)
bounds runs of integers *each sharing a factor with a fixed $N$*.
Here the modulus set $\{p : j < p \le n/2\}$ is not fixed: it is
defined by $n$ itself, its product is $\exp(\theta(n/2) - \theta(j))
\approx e^{n/2 - j}$, and a Chinese remainder construction of an
integer satisfying (b) for a *prescribed* set of primes produces a
residue class modulo that enormous product, i.e. an integer of size
$e^{(1+o(1))(n/2 - j)}$, at which point the prime set that must be
avoided has grown with it. The condition is self-referential, and
the usual covering-system bounds, which treat the moduli as given,
do not close it. This is the precise sense in which the problem is
"covering-system-flavoured" but not a covering system.

(iii) *Counting heuristic, and where it stops.* For a random integer
$n'$, the events at distinct primes are roughly independent with
$\Pr[n' \bmod p \ge i] = 1 - i/p$, so the proportion of $n'$
satisfying (b) for all $j < p \le Y$ is about
$\prod_{j < p \le Y} (1 - i/p) \approx \exp\big(-i\,(\log_2 Y -
\log_2 j)\big) = \big(\frac{\log j}{\log Y}\big)^i$ (Mertens). With
$Y = n/2$ this is $\approx 1$ when $i \ll \log n$ and $j$ is close
to $n/2$ in log scale, and it only becomes $o(1/n)$, the threshold
at which a counting argument could exclude even one bad $n' \le n$,
when $i \log\big(\frac{\log n}{\log j}\big) \gg \log n$. So even a
perfect sieve (Brun or large sieve upper bound for the count of bad
$n' \le X$, with the attendant error terms at level $Y \approx
X^{1/2}$) can at best operate in the regime $i \gg \log n$ with
$j \le n^{\beta}$, $\beta < 1/2$ bounded away: a plausible programme,
recorded here as a programme only, and one whose regime is largely
already covered by PC3 and PC5. For $i = O(\log n)$ and $j \approx
n/2$ the heuristic says bad residues are *common*; what saves the
conjecture there is not (b) at all but the primes in $[i, j]$ and
the size constraint $v \mid C(j, j-i)$, i.e. other routes.

(iv) *Least primes in residue classes do not apply either.* The
condition $n \bmod p < i$ for *some* $p > j$ is not a condition that
$n$ lie in a prescribed residue class modulo a prescribed modulus:
both the modulus $p$ and the target residue set $\{0, \dots, i-1\}$
move with the unknown. Linnik-type results (least prime in an
arithmetic progression) concern the dual picture and give no
purchase here; I flag this because it is a natural first guess.

## 6. Breaking point

**The regime.** Small $i$ (fixed, or growing slower than $\log n$),
with $j$ comparable to $n/2$. There $P^+(C(n,i)) \le j$ is not a
pathology but the generic, and sometimes forced, behaviour, while
every $P^+$ theorem gives a bound in terms of $i$ (at best about
$i \log i$, PC3 and PC5) or in terms of $n$ only when $i$ is a
positive power of $n$ (PC4). Since $j$ can be as large as $n/2$ with
$n$ independent of $i$, the gap between what anatomy forces
($\approx i \log i$) and what must be beaten ($j \approx n/2$) is
unbounded.

**Verified examples (Python standard library, exact Legendre
factorization of the binomials; recomputed for this paper).**

- $i = 1$, the clean infinite family: $n = 2^m$, $j = 2^{m-1} =
  n/2$. Then $C(n,1) = n = 2^m$ and $P^+ = 2 \le j$ for all
  $m \ge 2$, with $j / P^+ = 2^{m-2} \to \infty$. The conjecture
  holds, but only through the prime $2$, which is invisible to any
  "$p > j$" or even "$p \ge$ large" argument. The assigned
  $n = \mathrm{lcm}(1, \dots, m)$ family behaves the same way at
  $i = 1$: e.g. $n = \mathrm{lcm}(1,\dots,9) = 2520 =
  2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7$, $P^+ = 7$, against $j$ up to
  $1260$ (computed).
- $i = 2$: $(n, i) = (25, 2)$, $C(25,2) = 300 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot
  5^2$, $P^+ = 5$, against $j$ up to $12$. The working prime is $5$
  itself, well inside $[i, j]$. A sharper computed example of the
  same shape: $(n, i) = (1716, 2)$, $C(1716,2) = 1471470 =
  2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7^3 \cdot 11 \cdot 13$, so $P^+ = 13$
  against $j$ up to $858$, a ratio $j / P^+ = 66$; the common primes
  with $C(1716, 858)$ include $3, 5, 7, 11$ (computed), all deep
  inside $[i, j]$.
- $i = 3$: $(n, i) = (50, 3)$, $C(50,3) = 19600 = 2^4 \cdot 5^2
  \cdot 7^2$, $P^+ = 7$, against $j$ up to $25$ (in particular the
  open-region value $j = 25 > 3i/2$; the common prime with
  $C(50,25)$ is $7$, computed). Even the *largest* prime factor
  here is less than a third of $j$.

These are not counterexamples; they are counterexamples to the
*sufficient condition* $P^+(C(n,i)) > j$, showing the condition, and
hence this route in isolation, has a hard ceiling.

**Quantification of the ceiling.** For fixed $i$ and $j \to \infty$
with $n = 2j$, $C(2j, i)$ is a polynomial in $j$ of degree $i$ whose
prime factors are under no obligation to exceed $j$ (the $i = 1$
family shows all of them can be as small as $2$). Hence *no*
statement of the form $P^+(C(n,i)) > B(i)$ with $B$ depending only
on $i$ can close any $j \ge B(i)$, and $B(i)$ itself cannot exceed
the PC3/PC5 scale $O(i \log i)$ in general (the Jia-Liu range shows
$i^{1.82}$ is forcible only when $n$ is at most about $i^2$; for
$n \gg i^2$ the surveys give nothing beyond $i \log i$ times
iterated logs, and the smooth top blocks in (c) of Section 5 show
why: for fixed $i$, blocks of $i$ consecutive $j$-smooth integers
with $j$ fixed are finite in number by Størmer-type $S$-unit
finiteness, but with $j$ growing like $n/2$ smoothness at level $j$
is a weak condition, satisfied by a positive proportion of blocks).

**What the leftover regime needs (extra idea, stated plainly).**
The uncovered core is, up to the curves in Section 4:
$i$ below the PC3 threshold $i_0$, or $j$ above $c_0 i \log i
\frac{\log_2 i}{\log_3 i}$ with $n < i\pi(i) + i$ (so PC5(i) is
unavailable and PC5(ii) only partly compensates), and
$i \le n^{0.525}$ (so PC4 is unavailable); the extremal shape is
$i \approx \log n$, $j \approx n/2$. In that core a proof cannot
come from a single large prime. It must use either (1) the primes
in $[i, j]$ themselves, comparing $v_p(C(n,i))$ and $v_p(C(n,j))$
carry by carry in base $p$ (for $p \in (i, j]$ the expansions of
$i$ and $j$ are both one or two digits, so this is a finite
digit-pattern analysis per $p$, but it must be won for *some* $p$
simultaneously over all $p$), or (2) the full size strength of
$v \mid C(j, j-i)$ together with $v > C(n,i)^{1/2}$, i.e. the
project's reduction pushed beyond the per-pair finiteness it
currently gives, or (3) a second-moment version of PC5: not just
the count of distinct large primes but their total logarithmic
mass $\log v$ compared against $\log C(j, j-i)$ with the
EEES78 lower bound on $v$ as the input, in ranges where
$\omega$ alone is too weak. S9's contribution to that core is the
equivalent forms (b), (c), (d) of Section 5, which are the natural
interface for routes (1) and (3).
